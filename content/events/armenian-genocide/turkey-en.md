The Turkish government acknowledges that large numbers of Ottoman Armenians died in 1915 during the First World War and calls it a tragedy, but it consistently denies that these events constituted a state-directed policy of extermination, that is, "genocide." The official account holds that the **Tehcir (Relocation) Law of May 1915** was a wartime security measure against Armenian armed groups that allegedly cooperated with the invading Russian army, and that deaths resulted from famine, disease, bandit and tribal attacks, and the chaos of war. Ankara stresses that **hundreds of thousands** of Muslim civilians also died in the same period and calls the Armenian figure of **1.5 million** deaths exaggerated.

Turkish textbooks present the "Armenian Question" within the story of great-power intervention and nationalist movements that broke up the empire. In 2005 Prime Minister Erdoğan proposed a **joint historians' commission** and urged opening archives. In 2014 he expressed condolences to the descendants of the victims, yet still rejects the word "genocide." Domestically, Article 301 of the Penal Code restricts speech, and journalist **Hrant Dink**, editor of *Agos*, was assassinated in 2007. A number of historians, including Turkish scholars such as Taner Akçam, nevertheless characterize the events as genocide, in contrast to the official line.
