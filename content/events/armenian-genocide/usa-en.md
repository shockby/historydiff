For decades the US executive branch, wary of straining its NATO alliance with Turkey, avoided the word "genocide." Presidential statements on 24 April typically used terms such as "**Meds Yeghern**" or mass killing and deportation. The turning point came in **2019**, when the House (H.Res.296) and the Senate (S.Res.150) each passed resolutions formally recognizing the Armenian Genocide. On **24 April 2021** President Biden became the first sitting president to use the word explicitly.

The United States has a large Armenian-American community, and many state legislatures had already passed recognition measures. Mainstream US historians and genocide scholars treat the events as a paradigmatic genocide, relying heavily on diplomatic records in the National Archives, including Ambassador Henry Morgenthau's reports from Constantinople. Turkey strongly protested the recognition as interference and the politicization of history, including a summoning of the US ambassador. The White House stressed that the declaration is political and not legally binding.
