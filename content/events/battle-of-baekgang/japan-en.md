In the mid-7th century, the expansion of the Tang dynasty reshaped East Asian geopolitics. Allying with the Korean kingdom of Silla, the Tang launched a joint offensive that captured Sabi, the capital of Baekje, leading to the collapse of Baekje in 660 AD. Loyalist resistance forces led by General Gwisil Boksin and the monk Dochim rallied to restore the kingdom. They invited Prince Buyeo Pung, who had been living as an honored guest in Yamato (ancient Japan), to return as king and petitioned Yamato for urgent, comprehensive military intervention.

Empress Saimei and Crown Prince Naka no Oe (later Emperor Tenji) viewed the preservation of Baekje as vital for Yamato's strategic security and its longstanding political influence in the southern Korean peninsula. Yamato mobilized an unprecedented national effort. Although Empress Saimei passed away during the forward expedition in northern Kyushu, Crown Prince Naka no Oe governed in interim succession and dispatched over 40,000 troops and more than 400 warships to the peninsula across three separate naval waves between 661 and 663 AD.

In August 663 AD, the combined Yamato and Baekje restoration forces clashed with Tang naval forces commanded by General Liu Rengui (approximately 170 warships) and allied Silla land troops at the Baekgang estuary (near the mouth of modern-day Geum River). According to the *Nihon Shoki*, Yamato commanders grew overconfident, assuming their sheer numbers would force an enemy retreat without maintaining strict battle formation. The veteran Tang fleet outmaneuvered them, executing a coordinated pincer attack with fire tactics. Yamato's fleet suffered total destruction; chronicles recorded that the sky was scorched with smoke and flames, and the sea turned red with blood. Prince Buyeo Pung fled to Goguryeo, and the Baekje restoration movement was decisively extinguished.

The catastrophe triggered existential panic across Yamato. Anticipating an imminent retaliatory invasion by the Tang-Silla alliance, Crown Prince Naka no Oe accelerated sweeping national defense measures. Border guards (*Sakimori*) and signal beacons (*Tobuhi*) were positioned across Tsushima, Iki, and Kyushu. Massive defensive earthworks, including the Mizuki water fortress along Hakata Bay and Korean-style mountain castles (Ono Castle, Kii Castle, Nagato Castle), were constructed across western Japan with the assistance of exiled Baekje aristocrats and engineers. The imperial capital was relocated inland to Otsu in Omi, national household registers (*Kogo Nenjaku*) were compiled, and administrative centralization was accelerated.

In Japanese historiography and secondary education, the Battle of Baekgang is recognized as a pivotal catalyst that permanently ended ancient Japanese military intervention on the Korean peninsula and galvanized the consolidation of a centralized imperial bureaucracy, cementing the adoption of the national name "Nihon" (Japan) and the formal imperial title of Tenno.
