According to the official Chinese position, Tibet, Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia and the Northeast have each been inseparable parts of China since ancient times. For Tibet, Beijing says successive central governments exercised sovereignty from the 13th-century Yuan dynasty onward, and that the 1951 Seventeen Point Agreement brought "peaceful liberation" and ended feudal serfdom. The 1959 uprising is described as a revolt by the serf-owning class, and the 14th Dalai Lama as the head of a separatist exile clique.

For Xinjiang, China points to the Western Regions Protectorate established by the Han dynasty in 60 BCE and to the Qing creation of Xinjiang Province in 1884. "East Turkestan" is portrayed as a concept invented by separatists abroad with no historical basis. After a wave of terrorist attacks from 2014, the "vocational education and training centers" are described as counter-terrorism and de-radicalization measures whose trainees had all "graduated" by the end of 2019. Allegations of genocide and forced labor are rejected as "the lie of the century."

In Inner Mongolia, China stresses that an autonomous government was established in 1947 under Communist leadership, before the founding of the PRC in 1949, making it the first ethnic autonomous region. As for the Northeast (former Manchuria), the Manchus are one of 56 recognized ethnic groups and no sovereignty dispute exists; Manchukuo is regarded as a Japanese puppet state.

Beijing insists these are purely internal affairs in which foreign interference is unacceptable. It describes the 2020 introduction of national-language (Mandarin) teaching materials in Inner Mongolia as national-unity education, says minority language rights are guaranteed by the Constitution and the Regional Ethnic Autonomy Law, and in 2025 reiterated that the Dalai Lama's reincarnation requires the Golden Urn process and central government approval.
