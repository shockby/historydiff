The Tibetan exile body, the Central Tibetan Administration (Dharamshala, India), holds that Tibet was de facto independent from 1913 to 1950 and that the 1951 Seventeen Point Agreement was signed by a delegation without full authority under duress. Yet the official policy of the 14th Dalai Lama and the CTA is not independence but the "Middle Way Approach": genuine autonomy within China. Groups such as the Tibetan Youth Congress demand full independence. More than 150 self-immolations have been reported since 2009.

On the Uyghur side, the World Uyghur Congress (Munich) seeks democracy and self-determination, while others such as the "East Turkistan Government in Exile" (declared in Washington in 2004) call for independence. They cite the short-lived East Turkestan Republics of 1933 and 1944–49 and denounce mass internment, forced sterilization, forced labor and repression of religion and language in Xinjiang. The leaked 2022 "Xinjiang Police Files" were widely cited as evidence about the camps.

In Inner Mongolia, overseas groups such as the Southern Mongolian Democratic Alliance call for self-determination and cultural rights. In autumn 2020, policies reducing Mongolian-language instruction triggered school boycotts and protests, with many reportedly detained. The independence movement is small; the main focus is protecting language and culture. The government of Mongolia does not claim Inner Mongolia.

For Manchuria (Northeast China), no significant movement contests sovereignty. The roughly 10 million Manchus have a critically endangered language, and only some scholars and cultural groups advocate preserving it. In short, "dissent" varies greatly by region, mixing demands for independence, high autonomy, and human-rights and cultural protection.
