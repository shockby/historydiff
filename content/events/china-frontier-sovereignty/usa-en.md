Under its "One China" policy, the U.S. government formally recognizes Tibet and Xinjiang as part of the PRC and does not support independence for either. It has nonetheless sharply criticized Beijing over human rights, religion and culture. For Tibet, Congress passed the Reciprocal Access to Tibet Act (2018), the Tibetan Policy and Support Act (2020) and the Resolve Tibet Act (2024), calling for a negotiated solution.

On Xinjiang, Secretary of State Pompeo determined in January 2021 that China was committing genocide and crimes against humanity, a finding the Biden administration maintained. The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (2021) created a presumptive ban on imports from Xinjiang. Parliaments in Canada, the Netherlands and the UK adopted similar motions, though few governments have made the same determination.

The UN human rights office (OHCHR) said in August 2022 that arbitrary detention and other abuses in Xinjiang "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity." In October 2022 the Human Rights Council rejected a motion to debate the issue by 17 to 19, with many Muslim-majority and Global South states siding with China. In UN forums, more states have often signed pro-China joint statements than critical ones.

Western states thus generally accept sovereignty but challenge conduct. No UN member state disputes the territorial status itself. Japan's government has expressed concern over conditions in Xinjiang, Tibet and Inner Mongolia without addressing territorial attribution.
