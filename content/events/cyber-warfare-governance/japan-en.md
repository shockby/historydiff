The Japanese government defines cyberspace as a shared global commons driving economic growth and social innovation, consistently championing a "**free, fair, and secure cyberspace**" governed through a **multi-stakeholder model** involving the private sector, academia, and civil society. In international law, Japan is a party to the **2001 Budapest Convention on Cybercrime** and strongly endorses the **2015 UN Group of Governmental Experts (UN GGE)** consensus that **existing international law—including the UN Charter and international humanitarian law—applies in full to cyberspace**. Consequently, Tokyo has opposed state-centric "cyber sovereignty" models promoted by China and Russia and voiced concern over new UN treaty provisions that could lack human rights safeguards or legitimize domestic censorship.

At the same time, Japan's threat perception has darkened markedly since the late 2010s. Official statements by the Ministry of Defense, the National Police Agency, and NISC have increasingly joined the United States, the United Kingdom, and South Korea in issuing **joint public attributions** against state-sponsored actors. These include China-linked groups such as **MirrorFace**, **Tick**, and **BlackTech** targeting the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (**JAXA**) and defense contractors for cyber espionage; Russian military intelligence (GRU) units conducting hybrid infrastructure attacks and ransomware operations such as the **July 2023 shutdown of the Port of Nagoya container terminal**; and North Korea-linked groups (**Lazarus** / **TraderTraitor**) stealing cryptocurrency to fund WMD programs, including the **May 2024 theft of ¥48.2 billion ($305 million) from DMM Bitcoin**.

Historically, strict constitutional protections for the "**secrecy of communications**" under Article 21(2) of the Japanese Constitution and an exclusively defense-oriented posture prevented Japanese authorities from monitoring traffic metadata in peacetime or neutralizing foreign command-and-control servers—drawing concern from Western allies. To close this gap, Japan's **December 2022 National Security Strategy** pledged to introduce "**Active Cyber Defense (ACD)**" on par with major Western powers. In **2025**, Japan enacted landmark Active Cyber Defense legislation authorizing mandatory incident reporting for critical infrastructure, government analysis of foreign-routed communication metadata under independent oversight, and police/Self-Defense Forces operations to access and neutralize attacker servers during severe state-backed incidents.
