Rather than the narrower Western concept of technical "cybersecurity," the Russian government operates under a broader "**Information Security**" (*Informatsionnaya bezopasnost*) doctrine (adopted in 2000 and revised in 2016), which encompasses both technical network protection and the defense of Russia's socio-political stability, historical memory, and domestic information space against foreign psychological operations. Since introducing the first UN General Assembly resolution on information security in **1998**, Moscow has consistently championed **legally binding international treaties under UN auspices** based on state sovereignty and non-interference. Russia refuses to join the Western-led **2001 Budapest Convention**, arguing that Article 32(b)—which permits transborder access to stored computer data without prior state authorization—constitutes an **unacceptable violation of national sovereignty**. Instead, Russia spearheaded the creation of the UN Open-Ended Working Group (**OEWG**) and led the drafting of the **United Nations Convention against Cybercrime**, adopted by the UN General Assembly in **December 2024**. Domestically, Russia enacted the **2019 "Sovereign Internet" (Runet) Law** to ensure Russian networks can operate autonomously if disconnected by Western states.

Moscow categorically rejects Western **public attributions** linking Russian military intelligence (**GRU / Sandworm / APT28**) and foreign intelligence (**SVR / APT29**) to landmark incidents—including the **2007 Estonia DDoS attacks**, the **2015–2016 Ukrainian power grid blackouts**, the **2016 DNC hack**, the **2017 NotPetya outbreak**, and the **2020 SolarWinds intrusion**. The Russian Foreign Ministry dismisses such "megaphone diplomacy" as **unsubstantiated "Russophobia"** designed to justify unilateral sanctions, noting that Washington and its allies refuse to submit forensic evidence through bilateral legal channels.

Following the launch of its military campaign in Ukraine in **February 2022**, Russia's Federal Security Service (FSB) and National Coordination Center for Computer Incidents (NKTsKI) have accused the United States, NATO intelligence agencies, and Western tech corporations (such as Microsoft) of waging an **unprecedented proxy cyber war** against Russian civilian banking, energy, transport, and government infrastructure through the Ukrainian government-directed "**IT Army of Ukraine**." In Moscow's view, this proves that Western rhetoric about "protecting peacetime critical infrastructure" is selectively discarded whenever it suits Western geopolitical objectives.
