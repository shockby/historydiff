Cyprus gained independence from Britain in 1960 under a constitution designed to balance power between its Greek Cypriot majority and Turkish Cypriot minority. The arrangement quickly proved fragile. In 1963, President Makarios proposed constitutional amendments that Greek Cypriots regarded as necessary reforms but Turkish Cypriots saw as an attempt to strip them of guaranteed protections. Intercommunal violence erupted and continued intermittently until 1974, forcing roughly 25,000 Turkish Cypriots into UN-monitored enclaves where freedom of movement and economic participation were severely curtailed. This period is foundational to how each community understands the conflict: for Greek Cypriots it was civil unrest requiring policing; for Turkish Cypriots it was a decade of fear, displacement, and effective ghettoization that made external intervention feel existentially necessary.

The crisis came to a head in July 1974, when a coup engineered by the Greek military junta installed a hardline regime in Nicosia with the explicit aim of achieving enosis — union with Greece. Turkey, invoking its rights as a guarantor power under the 1960 Treaty of Guarantee, launched a military intervention on 20 July (Operation Atilla I) and, after a brief ceasefire collapsed, a far larger offensive in mid-August (Operation Atilla II) that brought roughly 37 percent of the island under Turkish military control. The human consequences were shattering: approximately 200,000 Greek Cypriots fled or were expelled from the north; approximately 45,000 Turkish Cypriots moved from south to north. For Greek Cypriots, this constitutes an illegal occupation under international law, a position supported by UN Security Council resolutions, multiple European Court of Human Rights judgments finding Turkey in breach of the European Convention, and the absence of international recognition for the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus (TRNC), declared in 1983 and recognized only by Ankara.

Turkish Cypriots frame 1974 very differently. The intervention, they insist, was a lawful "peace operation" that stopped an ongoing atrocity — the junta-backed coup — from spiralling into the systematic elimination of their community. Far from being an aggressor, Turkey was fulfilling a treaty obligation to restore the constitutional order it had guaranteed. The TRNC, established in 1983, represents the culmination of decades of Turkish Cypriot aspirations for self-governance free from a Greek Cypriot-controlled state that had never genuinely shared power. Turkish Cypriots point to the 2004 Annan Plan referendum as the clearest evidence of their good faith: 76 percent voted yes to a UN-brokered reunification proposal, while 76 percent of Greek Cypriots voted no — yet it was the Republic of Cyprus that joined the EU days later, inheriting benefits that were to have been conditional on a settlement. Within the Turkish Cypriot community, pragmatic voices favour negotiated reunification, while others, particularly those whose livelihoods depend on TRNC institutions or who arrived as settlers from mainland Turkey, resist solutions that might dissolve the north's separate identity.

The property issue cuts to the heart of the dispute. Some 200,000 Greek Cypriots lost homes, farms, and businesses in the north; an equal number of Turkish Cypriots left property behind in the south. The European Court of Human Rights ruled in Loizidou v. Turkey (1996) and subsequent cases that Turkey bears responsibility for ongoing violations of property rights in the north. The Republic of Cyprus established an Immovable Property Commission in 2006 — the body created partly in response to these rulings — but Greek Cypriot civil society groups largely regard it as inadequate. Meanwhile, tens of thousands of settlers brought from mainland Turkey after 1974 have built lives in formerly Greek Cypriot homes and towns, creating a further layer of demographic and legal complexity that any settlement must address. Reunification talks have stalled repeatedly — most recently at the Crans-Montana conference in 2017 — and the Green Line, patrolled by UNFICYP peacekeepers since 1974, remains one of the last hard partitions in Europe.
