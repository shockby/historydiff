The United States' response to Turkey's 1974 military intervention in Cyprus has been a source of lasting controversy. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger's perceived acquiescence to — or even tacit approval of — the Turkish operation, motivated by a desire to maintain stability with the key NATO ally Turkey following the collapse of the Greek junta, drew sharp criticism from Greek-Americans and human rights advocates. While Congress subsequently imposed a brief arms embargo on Turkey, the executive branch consistently sought to manage relations with Ankara as a strategic priority, often at the expense of more forceful advocacy for Cyprus's territorial integrity. This fundamental tension — between supporting international law and accommodating a vital alliance partner — has defined the American approach for five decades.

The United States was a strong proponent of the 2004 Annan Plan for reunification, working closely with the UN and the UK to bring both communities to agreement. When 76% of Greek Cypriots rejected the plan in a referendum — even as 65% of Turkish Cypriots approved it — American and British officials expressed frustration, and English-language media widely criticized the Greek Cypriot leadership under President Papadopoulos for campaigning against the plan. Cyprus's accession to the EU occurred shortly after, with the northern part of the island excluded from the acquis communautaire. Critics of the American position noted that the Annan Plan contained provisions that many Greek Cypriots viewed as skewed in Turkey's favor, and that the "no" vote reflected legitimate concerns rather than mere intransigence.

Contemporary American interest in Cyprus is shaped significantly by energy geopolitics. The discovery of substantial natural gas reserves in Cyprus's Exclusive Economic Zone — including the Aphrodite field and others — has elevated Cyprus's strategic importance in Washington's Eastern Mediterranean policy. In 2022, the Biden administration partially lifted the arms embargo on Cyprus that had been in place since 1987, citing Cyprus's cooperation in denying port services to vessels carrying sanctioned cargo. English-language media often describes the Cyprus dispute as a "forgotten conflict," but it remains highly consequential for NATO cohesion (given ongoing Greek-Turkish tensions), EU institutional integrity, and Eastern Mediterranean energy security — dimensions that ensure continued American strategic attention.
