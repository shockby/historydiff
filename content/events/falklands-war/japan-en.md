Japan's response to the 1982 Falklands War was shaped by its alliance with the United States and its broader commitment to the international rule of law, while also navigating its relationship with Latin American nations.

When Argentina invaded the Falkland Islands on 2 April 1982, Japan aligned itself with the Western bloc in condemning the action. The Japanese government expressed strong support for UN Security Council Resolution 502, which demanded the immediate withdrawal of Argentine forces, and called on Argentina to resolve the dispute peacefully through diplomatic means. Japan's position was that the use of force to resolve territorial disputes was fundamentally incompatible with the principles of the UN Charter and the norms of international law, regardless of the underlying historical merits of either side's claim.

Economically, Japan joined the European Community nations in imposing trade sanctions against Argentina following the invasion. Japanese banks suspended new loans to Argentina, and several bilateral economic projects were put on hold. Japan also introduced import restrictions on Argentine goods in solidarity with its Western allies, particularly the United Kingdom, with which Japan maintained close diplomatic and economic ties.

However, Japan's response was notably more measured than that of its European partners. As a nation that relies heavily on Latin American countries for resources and trade, and given Japan's own historical experience of militarism and its constitutional commitment to peaceful resolution of disputes, Japan tried to avoid antagonizing Argentina unnecessarily. Japan also quietly advocated for early diplomatic settlement throughout the conflict, stressing its view that dialogue and negotiation were the only sustainable path to a lasting solution. After the conflict ended, Japan moved swiftly to normalize its economic relations with Argentina.
