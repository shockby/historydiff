The United Kingdom's position is unequivocal: the Falkland Islands are a British Overseas Territory, and their sovereignty belongs to the United Kingdom based on both historical title and the democratic principle of self-determination.

Britain first formally claimed the Falkland Islands in 1765, establishing a settlement at Port Egmont on West Falkland. Although Britain temporarily withdrew in 1774 for economic reasons, it explicitly reserved its sovereign rights in writing. Argentina's claim, inherited from Spain, rests on the brief and disputed presence of a Buenos Aires administration from 1820 to 1833, which Britain never recognized. In January 1833, British forces reasserted control over the islands, and a continuous British administration has existed ever since.

The Argentine military junta, facing severe domestic political and economic crises, launched an illegal invasion of the Falkland Islands on 2 April 1982. UN Security Council Resolution 502, passed the same day, demanded an immediate ceasefire and the withdrawal of all Argentine forces. Argentina's refusal to comply with this binding UN resolution, combined with its act of naked aggression, left the United Kingdom with no choice but to dispatch a naval task force to restore British sovereignty.

The 74-day war ended with the Argentine surrender on 14 June 1982. The decisive factor affirming British legitimacy, beyond the military outcome, is the principle of self-determination enshrined in the UN Charter. The islanders, known as the Falkland Islanders, are overwhelmingly and consistently British in their identity and democratic wish. In the March 2013 sovereignty referendum, 99.8% of voters chose to remain a British Overseas Territory. The United Kingdom will continue to honour that democratic mandate and defend the islands' security.
