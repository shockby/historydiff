The United States' position during the 1982 Falklands War was shaped by a fundamental tension between two of its core strategic interests: its special relationship and NATO alliance with the United Kingdom, and the Monroe Doctrine tradition of hemispheric solidarity with Latin American nations under the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance (the Rio Treaty).

When Argentina invaded in April 1982, the Reagan administration initially attempted to mediate the dispute. Secretary of State Alexander Haig undertook a series of intensive shuttle diplomacy missions between London and Buenos Aires in April 1982, attempting to broker a solution that would allow Argentina to save face while securing British sovereignty. When those efforts collapsed, the United States publicly sided with the United Kingdom. Washington confirmed that Argentina had committed an act of aggression and agreed to provide the British with crucial military intelligence, satellite data, logistical support, and access to the Ascension Island airbase. The US also imposed economic sanctions on Argentina.

The decision to back Britain was controversial within the Reagan administration. U.N. Ambassador Jeane Kirkpatrick and others advocated neutrality or support for Argentina, arguing that alienating Latin America would damage US strategic interests and that Argentina's authoritarian government was a valuable Cold War partner against communist influence in South America. Reagan ultimately rejected this view, siding with National Security Advisor William Clark and the UK. The President justified this decision by arguing that the principle of non-aggression and the defense of a democratic ally outweighed short-term hemisphere politics.

The US decision to back Britain severely damaged its relationships with several Latin American nations and exposed the limits of the Rio Treaty as a framework for hemispheric solidarity. In the long term, the conflict discredited Argentina's military junta and accelerated the country's return to democracy, an outcome the United States welcomed. The episode demonstrated both the depth of the Anglo-American special relationship and the complexities of US strategic interests in an era of Cold War competition.
