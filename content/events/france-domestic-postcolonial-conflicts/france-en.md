Grounded in Article 1 of the 1958 Constitution, the French government upholds the model of an “**indivisible, secular (*laïque*), democratic, and social Republic**” that recognizes only equal individual citizens before the law, rejecting official ethnic, racial, or religious categories. Domestic unrest in suburban housing estates (**banlieues**)—such as the 2005 riots and the **June–July 2023 unrest** triggered by the fatal police shooting of 17-year-old Nahel Merzouk—is addressed by prioritizing republican order alongside urban renewal programs, educational investment, and the **2021 Law Reinforcing Respect for the Principles of the Republic** aimed at countering Islamist “separatism.”

Regarding Corsica and overseas territories (**Outre-mer**), Paris emphasizes constitutional legality and democratic referendums. In **New Caledonia**, France conducted three independence referendums under the **1998 Nouméa Accord** (in 2018, 2020, and **December 2021**), all of which returned majorities in favor of remaining French. When deadly riots erupted in **May 2024** over a constitutional bill to unfreeze provincial voting rolls, Paris deployed security forces to restore order, subsequently suspended the reform, and brokered the **July 2025 Bougival Accord**, proposing a new status as a “State of New Caledonia” within the French Republic. In **Corsica**, following 2022 protests over the prison killing of nationalist Yvan Colonna, the government reached a **2024 constitutional draft agreement** recognizing Corsica's “autonomous status within the Republic.”

On colonial history, President Emmanuel Macron has pursued a policy of “**truth and reconciliation of memories**” informed by historian **Benjamin Stora's 2021 report** on the **Algerian War (1954–1962)** and the **2025 Franco-Cameroonian joint historical commission report**. Macron has officially acknowledged systematic French military torture and the killings of Maurice Audin (2018) and Ali Boumendjel (2021), condemned the **October 17, 1961 Paris crackdown**, formally recognized the **1944 Thiaroye killing** of West African riflemen in Senegal as a “massacre” (November 2024), and acknowledged France's violent suppression of independence movements in **Cameroon (1955–1971)**—while explicitly ruling out formal state “repentance” (apology) or financial reparations. In Africa, Paris declares that the era of **Françafrique** is over, framing the withdrawal of French military bases across the Sahel and West Africa between 2022 and 2025 as a transition toward balanced, sovereign partnerships.
