During the August 2008 Russo-Georgian War, China maintained calculated strategic silence, neither endorsing Russia's recognition of Abkhazia and South Ossetia nor condemning Russia's military action. The Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs called for "all parties to exercise restraint and ease tensions," stopping well short of criticizing Moscow. At the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) summit held days after the war, China signed a declaration expressing "understanding" of Russia's position in South Ossetia, but carefully refrained from explicitly supporting the recognition of the two territories as independent states. This calibrated ambiguity allowed Beijing to signal solidarity with its strategic partner while avoiding a formal commitment that could create uncomfortable precedents.

Beijing's caution was grounded in its own ethno-territorial sensitivities. China faces separatist pressures in Tibet, Xinjiang, and the ongoing Taiwan question — all contexts in which the principle of self-determination could be weaponized against Chinese interests. Russia's attempt to legitimize the recognition of Abkhazia and South Ossetia by invoking the Kosovo precedent (the 2008 declaration of Kosovo's independence, which China had strongly opposed) placed Beijing in an awkward position. The "Kosovo precedent" argument that Russia deployed was precisely the logic China had resisted when Western powers recognized Kosovo, creating an ironic alignment of interests between Beijing and Belgrade on opposing self-determination while leaving China uncomfortable with Russia's application of the same principle in the Caucasus.

Within the framework of the China-Russia "strategic partnership," China has continued to support Russia diplomatically on key issues while formally maintaining support for Georgia's territorial integrity as a stated principle. China has economic interests in Georgia — viewing the South Caucasus as an important western corridor within the Belt and Road Initiative — and has cultivated bilateral trade and infrastructure ties with Tbilisi. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 deepened the China-Russia strategic alignment, Beijing has become even less inclined to criticize Russian policy in the Caucasus. Georgia's ruling Georgian Dream party's increasingly pro-Russian orientation in 2024, including its suspension of EU accession talks, is a development that, while criticized by the West, does not displease Beijing.
