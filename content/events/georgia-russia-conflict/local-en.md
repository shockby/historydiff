From the Georgian perspective, Abkhazia and South Ossetia are unambiguously Georgian territories under international law and history — regions currently under Russian military occupation. Following Georgia's restoration of independence after the Soviet collapse in 1991, Russia systematically exploited former Soviet autonomous structures to foment secessionist movements and weaken the Georgian state. The 1992–93 Abkhazia War, prosecuted with direct and indirect Russian support, resulted in the expulsion of approximately 250,000 ethnic Georgians — predominantly Mingrelians and Svans — from their ancestral homes in a process widely recognized by international bodies as ethnic cleansing. These internally displaced persons remain unable to return more than three decades later. Georgia's consistent position is that no political process can legitimize the results of ethnically motivated mass displacement.

Regarding the August 2008 Five-Day War, the Georgian government maintains that its military operation against Tskhinvali was a response to sustained provocation and intensifying artillery fire from South Ossetian militias. Georgian officials argue that international criticism focusing narrowly on who "fired first" ignores the broader context: Russia had been amassing troops near the border for weeks and actively inciting South Ossetian armed groups to provoke a Georgian response. Following Georgia's military withdrawal, Russian forces continued advancing deep into undisputed Georgian territory, temporarily occupying the city of Gori — actions that far exceeded any peacekeeping mandate. Russia's recognition of Abkhazia and South Ossetia as independent states on August 26, 2008 constitutes, in Georgia's view, an internationally illegal act that violates Article 2(4) of the UN Charter's prohibition on the acquisition of territory by force, a position upheld by an overwhelming majority of UN member states.

The Abkhazian majority narrative diverges sharply. Abkhazians contend that the 1992–93 war began with a Georgian military invasion of Abkhazia, and that Abkhazians were exercising their right to self-determination in a moment of Soviet dissolution, having already adopted a sovereignty declaration in the Abkhaz Supreme Soviet in 1990. The mass exodus of ethnic Georgians is framed not as deliberate ethnic cleansing but as the chaotic, bidirectional population displacement inevitable in any armed conflict — with Abkhazian civilians also displaced by Georgian forces. Russian military presence is viewed as a security guarantee against Georgian reconquest, not as occupation. Abkhazia points to the Kosovo precedent — where a region achieved internationally recognized independence over Serbian and Russian objections — as a parallel legitimizing its own claim to statehood, arguing that self-determination must apply universally or not at all.

For many South Ossetian residents, the roots of the conflict lie in the early 1990s policies of Georgian nationalist leader Zviad Gamsakhurdia, whose government abolished South Ossetia's autonomous oblast status, suppressed Ossetian cultural rights, and oversaw armed violence against the Ossetian population. The immediate prelude to the August 2008 war is remembered as a brutal Georgian artillery barrage that devastated Tskhinvali and killed civilians, making Russian intervention feel like a rescue operation to those who lived through it. Though South Ossetia today functions as a de facto Russian protectorate with minimal genuine sovereignty, the prospect of reintegration into Georgia is rejected across the political spectrum — an insistence rooted in historical grievance, ethnic identity, and the lived memory of the 1990s conflicts.
