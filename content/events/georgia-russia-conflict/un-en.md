From the perspective of international institutions — the United Nations, European Union, and OSCE — the Georgia conflict represents a collision of competing principles of international law in one of the post-Cold War era's most legally complex cases. The most authoritative multilateral assessment remains the 2009 Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on the Conflict in Georgia, commissioned by the EU and led by Swiss diplomat Heidi Tagliavini. The Tagliavini Report reached a nuanced but consequential set of findings: it concluded that Georgia initiated the military phase of the conflict, determining that the large-scale Georgian artillery attack on Tskhinvali on the night of August 7–8, 2008 was not justifiable under international law. At the same time, the report found that Russia's military response, while initially defensible under the rubric of protecting its citizens and peacekeepers, exceeded all reasonable bounds once Russian forces advanced beyond South Ossetia into undisputed Georgian territory — occupying Gori, Senaki, and Poti — making that phase of the Russian operation a violation of international law. The report also noted that South Ossetian militias had engaged in actions amounting to ethnic cleansing against Georgian villages during and after the conflict.

The UN Security Council held multiple emergency sessions following the August 2008 outbreak but was unable to pass binding resolutions, as Russia's veto power as a permanent member paralyzed the body. The UN General Assembly, however, passed Resolution A/RES/63/307 in May 2009 by a vote of 144 to 19 (with 23 abstentions), affirming the right of return of all internally displaced persons and refugees to Abkhazia and South Ossetia, including the approximately 250,000 ethnic Georgians displaced from Abkhazia in the early 1990s. The lopsided vote demonstrated the near-universal international rejection of Russian recognition. The UN's consistent position has been to uphold Georgia's territorial integrity within its internationally recognized borders, and UN Secretary-General statements have reiterated support for Georgian sovereignty throughout the post-2008 period.

The EU Monitoring Mission (EUMM Georgia) deployed in October 2008 as the sole international observation presence on the ground, tasked with monitoring the ceasefire lines and the situation in Georgian-controlled areas. However, EUMM monitors have been systematically denied access to the Abkhazian and South Ossetian sides of the Administrative Boundary Lines by Russian forces and de facto authorities — a critical limitation that prevents full situational awareness and undermines the mission's effectiveness. The OSCE Mission to Georgia, which had previously maintained a presence in South Ossetia, was dissolved in 2009 after Russia vetoed its mandate renewal. The UN Observer Mission in Georgia (UNOMIG), monitoring the Abkhazia ceasefire line, was similarly terminated by Russian veto in 2009. The withdrawal of both monitoring missions removed the last independent international observers from the conflict zones themselves.

The legal status of Russian recognition of Abkhazia and South Ossetia is overwhelmingly assessed by international legal scholars and UN bodies as a violation of Article 2(4) of the UN Charter, which prohibits the acquisition of territory by force or threats thereof. Russia has drawn parallels to the ICJ's 2010 advisory opinion on Kosovo's declaration of independence, which found that unilateral declarations of independence are not per se prohibited under international law. However, the dominant legal view distinguishes Kosovo — where the declaration followed a comprehensive UN-administered period and Security Council involvement — from Abkhazia and South Ossetia, where recognition came immediately from the same power that had just conducted military operations. With only five UN member states extending recognition, the de facto status of both regions is best described as internationally unrecognized entities in a protracted frozen conflict.
