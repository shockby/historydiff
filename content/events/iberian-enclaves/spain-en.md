Spain maintains that Ceuta and Melilla are integral parts of the Spanish state. Both cities have been under Spanish sovereignty for centuries—long before Morocco achieved independence in 1956—and Spain argues that calling them "colonies" is historically inaccurate. Ceuta was captured by Portugal in 1415, passed to Spain through the Iberian Union in 1580, and confirmed as Spanish territory by the Treaty of Lisbon in 1668. Melilla has been Spanish since 1497, predating the existence of Morocco as an independent nation-state. The residents of both cities hold Spanish nationality, are EU citizens, and identify as Spanish.

Regarding the Canary Islands, Spain asserts continuous sovereignty since the fifteenth-century conquest. The islands were formally recognized as Spanish territory by the Treaty of Alcáçovas (1479) and the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494). Today, the Canary Islands are an autonomous community of Spain and a full EU region. Spain argues that their geographic proximity to the African continent does not alter their administrative status.

On Gibraltar, Spain contends that Gibraltar is a Spanish historical territory, ceded to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. Spain has long sought its return, and the United Nations has recognized Gibraltar as a non-self-governing territory. Spain has pursued bilateral dialogue through pre-EU negotiations and the Brussels Process (1984), while Brexit has introduced new diplomatic complexities. Spain states that while it respects the interests of Gibraltar's current residents, the question of sovereignty should ultimately be resolved between Spain and the United Kingdom.

For Spain, the issues of Ceuta and Melilla on the one hand and Gibraltar on the other are asymmetrical. Spain maintains its sovereignty over its North African territories as legitimate while challenging British sovereignty over Gibraltar. Conscious of the criticism this perceived double standard attracts, Spain emphasizes the differing historical circumstances and legal bases of each case.
