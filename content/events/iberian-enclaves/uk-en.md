The United Kingdom's position is that Gibraltar is a British Overseas Territory whose status is maintained on the basis of the right to self-determination: the overwhelming majority of its inhabitants wish to remain British. Gibraltar was captured by a joint Anglo-Dutch fleet in 1704 and formally ceded by Spain to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. Continuous British governance has been maintained for more than three hundred years since.

Referendums held in 1967 and 2002 saw Gibraltar's residents vote by overwhelming margins against any transfer of sovereignty to Spain. In 1967, 99.6% chose to remain British; in 2002, 98.5% rejected a proposed Spanish-British "joint sovereignty" arrangement. The UK regards these democratic expressions of will as paramount and has explicitly stated that it has no intention of altering Gibraltar's sovereignty without the consent of its people.

On Ceuta and Melilla, the UK has generally maintained a neutral stance, neither formally supporting nor opposing Spain's position. In practice, however, the UK has tended to treat these as Spanish internal matters, reflecting its relationship with Spain as a fellow NATO ally. Since Brexit, Gibraltar has faced new negotiating challenges regarding access to the EU single market, and framework agreement talks with the EU have been ongoing since 2021.

The United Kingdom places the UN's "colonial" designation of Gibraltar—which appears on the UN list of Non-Self-Governing Territories—in a complex context. While the listing is acknowledged, the UK argues that from the standpoint of self-determination, the current status reflects the freely expressed will of the territory's people, and that any forced "decolonization" would violate their democratic rights. As for the Canary Islands, which are an autonomous community of Spain and a full constituent region of the EU, the UK regards this as a matter outside its purview.
