In the late 16th century, during the reign of the Wanli Emperor of the Ming Dynasty, Toyotomi Hideyoshi launched a massive invasion of the Korean peninsula. As the preeminent suzerain power of the East Asian tributary system, the Ming imperial court mobilized vast military forces to defend its tributary ally. In traditional Chinese official historiography (the *History of Ming*) and modern Chinese scholarship, this conflict is known as the *Wanli Korean Campaign* (*Wanli Chaoxian Zhi Yi*), *Resisting Japanese Aggression to Aid Korea* (*Kangwo Yuanzhao*), or the *Eastern Expedition*, ranked alongside the pacification of Ningxia and Bozhou as one of the "Three Great Campaigns of the Wanli Era" (*Wanli San Da Zheng*). For Ming China, Joseon was an indispensable buffer state bound by close ties ("as close as lips and teeth"); Hideyoshi's aggression was perceived as a direct geopolitical assault against the imperial capital of Beijing and the Confucian tributary world order.

Key perspectives and conclusions in Chinese historiography include:

First, **imperial suzerain duty and national security imperatives**. When desperate appeals arrived from King Seonjo, the imperial court initially harbored suspicions that Joseon might be colluding with Japan due to the astonishing speed of the Japanese advance. However, once border investigations by Liaodong officials confirmed Joseon's unwavering loyalty, the Wanli Emperor and Minister of War Shi Xing decisively committed the empire's forces. Aiding Korea was not only a moral duty of benevolent suzerainty (*zixiao*), but a crucial defensive strategy: intercepting Japanese forces on the Korean peninsula was imperative to keep war away from the strategic Liaodong corridor and the Ming capital.

Second, **the recapture of Pyongyang by Li Rusong and the Battle of Byeokjegwan**. In late 1592, Imperial Commissioner Song Yingchang and General Commander Li Rusong led 40,000 elite Liaodong cavalry equipped with advanced artillery (such as Frankish breech-loading cannons, *Folangji*) across the frozen Yalu River. In January 1593, allied Ming-Joseon forces stormed Pyongyang, overwhelming Konishi Yukinaga's division and recapturing the northern fortress. As Li advanced toward Hanseong, however, his vanguard met heavy resistance from entrenched Japanese samurai forces at the Battle of Byeokjegwan, resulting in tactical setback and dragging the war into a protracted stalemate.

Third, **diplomatic intrigue and the second eastern expedition**. Strained by logistical shortages, both sides engaged in deceptive peace negotiations orchestrated by Ming diplomat Shen Weijing and Konishi Yukinaga. Ming sought to resolve the crisis through classical tributary investiture, granting Hideyoshi the official seal of "King of Japan" without granting tributary trade rights. When Hideyoshi discovered he had received an imperial vassal title rather than territorial concessions, the negotiations collapsed. In response to Hideyoshi's 1597 second invasion, the Ming court mobilized over 100,000 troops under Supreme Commander Xing Jie, General Ma Gui, and Yang Hao. At the decisive Battle of Noryang Point in late 1598, Ming Admiral Chen Lin and Vice General Deng Zilong fought side-by-side with Admiral Yi Sun-sin; both Deng and Yi sacrificed their lives in the victorious naval engagement that annihilated the retreating Japanese fleet.

Fourth, **fiscal exhaustion and the catalyst for the Ming-Qing transition**. The seven-year war cost the Ming treasury an astronomical sum of silver (estimated at over 8 million taels) and severely depleted its premier Liaodong frontier armies. While celebrated as a righteous victory that upheld imperial prestige and preserved Korean sovereignty, the military and financial drain critically weakened Ming border garrisons in the northeast. This power vacuum allowed Nurhaci and the Jianzhou Jurchens to consolidate power in Manchuria, culminating in the rise of the Later Jin (Qing Dynasty) and ultimately precipitating the fall of the Ming Dynasty decades later. In Chinese historiography, the campaign is regarded both as an honorable defense of regional stability and as the pivotal turning point that accelerated the dynastic transition.
