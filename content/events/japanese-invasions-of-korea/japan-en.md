In the late 16th century, having completed the unification of Japan following the chaotic Sengoku (Warring States) period, Toyotomi Hideyoshi sought to dismantle the traditional Sinocentric tributary order and establish a regional hegemony centered on Japan. Hideyoshi demanded that the Joseon Dynasty of Korea grant passage and assist his intended conquest of Ming China ("Conquer Ming, Request Route"). When Joseon categorically refused, Hideyoshi launched an armada of over 158,000 troops across the Korea Strait in 1592. In Japan, these two campaigns are known as the Bun'ei and Keicho Campaigns (*Bunroku-Keicho no Eki*), the Japanese Invasions of Korea, or colloquially as *Kara-iri* ("Entering the Continent").

Key themes and perspectives in Japanese historiography and education include:

First, **the motivations and domestic dynamics of the invasion**. Japanese historians analyze a complex web of motivations behind Hideyoshi's aggressive expansionism: channeling the surplus military energy of a heavily militarized samurai class outward, satisfying feudal lords' demands for newly conquered fiefs (*onsho*), securing maritime trade hegemony amid expanding European Nanban trade and Christian influence, and Hideyoshi's imperial vision of relocating Emperor Go-Yozei to Beijing. A massive military headquarters, Nagoya Castle, was hastily constructed in Hizen Province (modern Saga Prefecture) to mobilize samurai contingents from across Japan.

Second, **rapid initial offensives, stalemate, and deceptive diplomacy**. In the first campaign (1592), vanguard forces commanded by Konishi Yukinaga and Kato Kiyomasa leveraged firearms (matchlock arquebuses) and battlefield experience to capture Hanseong (Seoul) within a month and push northward to Pyongyang and the Tumen River. However, logistics collapsed as Admiral Yi Sun-sin's naval victories severed maritime supply lines, Korean righteous armies (*uibyeong*) engaged in guerrilla warfare, and Ming China intervened militarily (notably at the Battle of Byeokjegwan). Field commanders such as Konishi and Ming envoy Shen Weijing attempted deceptive peace talks, misleading both monarchs; when Hideyoshi discovered Ming merely offered him the tributary title "King of Japan" without territorial concessions, talks violently collapsed.

Third, **war of attrition in the Keicho Campaign and full withdrawal**. In 1597, Hideyoshi dispatched a second expedition of 140,000 troops. Facing fortified Allied Ming-Joseon resistance and worsening supply shortages, Japanese forces were confined to coastal stone fortresses (*Wajo*) across the southern peninsula, enduring brutal sieges such as the Siege of Ulsan. Following Hideyoshi's death at Fushimi Castle in August 1598, the Council of Five Elders (led by Tokugawa Ieyasu) covertly ordered an immediate cessation of hostilities and a complete withdrawal.

Fourth, **political collapse at home and cultural legacy**. The disastrous campaigns severely depleted the military strength and finances of western daimyo loyal to the Toyotomi clan. Bitter recriminations between military commanders (such as Kato Kiyomasa) and administrative bureaucrats (such as Ishida Mitsunari) directly paved the way for the decisive Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and the establishment of the Tokugawa Shogunate. Concurrently, thousands of captured Korean ceramic craftsmen, Neo-Confucian scholars, and movable metal type brought to Japan transformed early modern Japanese culture—catalyzing famous porcelain traditions (Arita, Satsuma, Hagi wares) and Confucian scholarly development. In modern Japanese education, the campaigns are acknowledged as a reckless war of aggression that inflicted immense suffering upon the peoples of Korea and China.
