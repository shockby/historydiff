In 1592 (the 25th year of King Seonjo's reign, the year of Imjin), Toyotomi Hideyoshi unleashed a catastrophic, unprovoked invasion of the Korean peninsula with over 158,000 troops. In Korea, this conflict is known as the *Imjin War* (Imjin Waeran), and the 1597 second invasion as the *Jeongyu War* (Jeongyu Jaeran). Having enjoyed nearly two centuries of peace under the Joseon Dynasty, the nation suffered immense devastation, civilian massacres, and cultural plundering. Yet through the brilliant naval campaigns of Admiral Yi Sun-sin, the grassroots mobilization of "Righteous Armies" (*uibyeong*), and allied Ming military assistance, Joseon decisively repelled the invaders after seven years of brutal warfare.

Core themes in Korean historical consciousness and collective memory include:

First, **the existential shock of unprovoked aggression and early crisis**. The Japanese invasion began under the pretext of *Jeongmyeong Gado* ("Conquer Ming China, Demand Joseon Provide the Route"). Unprepared for total war against battle-hardened samurai wielding massed firearms, regular Joseon garrisons suffered swift collapses, and the capital Hanseong fell within weeks. King Seonjo was forced to flee north to Uiju on the Yalu River border. Ancient royal palaces such as Gyeongbokgung, sacred temples like Bulguksa, and countless national historical archives were incinerated or looted.

Second, **Admiral Yi Sun-sin and naval supremacy**. The paramount turning point of the war occurred at sea under the leadership of Naval Commander Yi Sun-sin. Employing innovative armored "turtle ships" (*Geobukseon*), superior naval cannonry, and sophisticated fleet maneuvers (such as the "Crane Wing" formation), Yi achieved an unbroken string of stunning victories at Okpo, Hansan Island, and the miraculous Battle of Myeongnyang—where his 13 ships routed over 133 Japanese warships. By controlling the southern and western seas, Yi severed Japanese maritime supply lines, starving Japanese land forces in the north and halting their advance. Admiral Yi remains revered throughout Korean history as the supreme national hero ("Seong-ung", the Sacred Hero).

Third, **grassroots uprising of Righteous Armies and monk soldiers**. When central authority faltered, civilians across the eight provinces took up arms. Neo-Confucian scholars and peasants rallied under charismatic leaders like Gwak Jae-u ("the Red-Robed General"), Jo Heon, and Go Gyeong-myeong, while Buddhist warrior monks led by master Hyujeong and Yujeong fought alongside them. Utilizing intimate knowledge of local mountain terrain, these guerrilla forces harassed enemy supply columns, ambushed detachments, and defended critical fortresses (such as the Siege of Jinju and the Battle of Haengju fortress led by General Gwon Yul), galvanizing national morale.

Fourth, **atrocities, war trauma, and the enduring lessons of Jingbirok**. The war inflicted apocalyptic destruction: cultivated farmland shrank to less than a third of pre-war levels, while famine and epidemics ravaged the civilian population. Japanese commanders infamously severed the noses and ears of tens of thousands of Korean combatants and civilians as proof of decapitation quotas, sending pickled trophies to Kyoto where the burial mound *Mimizuka* (Ears Mound) stands today. Tens of thousands of skilled ceramic artisans, scholars, and printers were forcibly abducted to Japan. Prime Minister Ryu Seong-ryong documented these tragic events and government failures in *Jingbirok* ("Book of Corrections", from the phrase "I shall admonish past errors to prevent future calamity") as a solemn warning to future generations. For Korea, the Imjin War remains the quintessential trial of national resilience and a foundational lesson in the necessity of sovereign self-defense.
