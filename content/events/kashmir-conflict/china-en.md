China's stance on the Kashmir issue is multifaceted, shaped by its complex bilateral relationships with both India and Pakistan. Officially, China maintains the general position that "Kashmir is a legacy issue between India and Pakistan that should be resolved through dialogue between the parties in accordance with UN resolutions," but the reality is considerably more nuanced.

The most critical factor is China's de facto control over Aksai Chin, a high-altitude plateau of approximately 37,000 square kilometers that India claims as part of its Union Territory of Ladakh. China regards Aksai Chin as Chinese sovereign territory dating back to the Qing dynasty and constructed the strategic Xinjiang-Tibet Highway (connecting Lhasa to Hotan) through the region in the 1950s. India did not discover the existence of this road until around 1957–1958, which contributed to the 1962 Sino-Indian War.

Equally significant is China's investment in infrastructure through Azad Kashmir (Pakistan-administered territory) and Gilgit-Baltistan as part of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC). India strongly objects, as the corridor passes through territory over which India claims sovereignty. CPEC is the flagship project of China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and carries enormous geopolitical significance.

In international discussions on Kashmir, China frequently issues statements supportive of Pakistan's position and has openly opposed India's actions, particularly the 2019 revocation of Article 370 and the reorganization of Ladakh into a Union Territory. China views the unilateral incorporation of Ladakh into India as a provocation against territories — including Aksai Chin — over which Beijing claims sovereignty.
