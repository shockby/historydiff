Pakistan frames Kashmir as its "unfinished agenda" — a fundamental piece of unresolved business from the 1947 partition — and asserts that the people of Kashmir have the right to exercise self-determination to decide their own political future. From Pakistan's perspective, the spirit of the Two-Nation Theory, which formed the basis of partition, dictates that Muslim-majority Kashmir should rightfully belong to Pakistan.

Pakistan contests the validity of Maharaja Hari Singh's Instrument of Accession to India in October 1947, arguing that it did not reflect the will of the Kashmiri people and that Pakistan already held treaty rights over Kashmir. Pakistan had signed a "Standstill Agreement" with the state of Jammu and Kashmir immediately after independence, and Islamabad maintains that the Maharaja's accession to India without consulting Pakistan was illegitimate. Furthermore, Pakistan consistently invokes UN Security Council resolutions — particularly Resolution 47 of 1948 — which called for a plebiscite to determine Kashmir's future, and Islamabad demands that such a plebiscite be held.

Regarding Azad Kashmir ("Free Kashmir"), the Pakistan-administered territory of the region, Pakistan administers the area but officially does not designate it as "part of Pakistan," framing it instead as a disputed territory — a stance meant to maintain consistency with its international commitment to a plebiscite across the entirety of Kashmir.

For Pakistan, India's revocation of Article 370 in 2019 was a blatant violation of UN resolutions and a unilateral attempt to alter the status quo in Indian-administered Kashmir. Pakistan consistently draws international attention to alleged human rights abuses in Indian-administered Kashmir and continues to seek to bring the issue before the United Nations and international human rights bodies, pushing against India's insistence on bilateral-only dialogue.
