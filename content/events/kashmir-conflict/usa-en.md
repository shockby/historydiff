The United States' position on Kashmir has evolved from the Cold War era to the present, but its consistent principle is to call for a peaceful resolution through dialogue between the parties concerned. Washington has generally avoided endorsing any specific sovereign outcome on the issue, prioritizing the maintenance of constructive relations with both India and Pakistan.

During the Cold War, the U.S. viewed Pakistan as a critical strategic ally — particularly in the context of containing the Soviet Union after its invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 — and provided substantial military assistance to Islamabad. India, meanwhile, was a leader of the Non-Aligned Movement, but Washington consistently avoided allowing Kashmir to become a definitive obstacle in U.S.-India relations. After both countries tested nuclear weapons in 1998, the U.S. also came to view the resolution of the Kashmir dispute as important in the context of nuclear non-proliferation.

Following the September 11 attacks, the U.S. assumed a complex posture: prioritizing relations with Pakistan as a partner in the Afghanistan War while simultaneously criticizing Pakistan's support for militant groups. From the 2010s onward, the strategic relationship between the U.S. and India deepened significantly, particularly in the framework of the "Quad" and the Indo-Pacific Strategy, as India's importance in counterbalancing China's rise grew.

When India revoked Article 370 in 2019, the U.S. government expressed "concerns" while broadly maintaining a tilt toward India. Former President Trump offered to "mediate" in July 2019 but was immediately rebuffed by India. Today, the U.S. increasingly values India as a strategic partner in countering China in the Indo-Pacific, though Washington continues to avoid officially endorsing any sovereign determination on the Kashmir question.
