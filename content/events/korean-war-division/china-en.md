In China, the Korean War is officially designated the "War to Resist America and Aid Korea" (抗美援朝战争, Kàngměi Yuáncháo Zhànzhēng). The decision by the newly founded People's Republic — established only in October 1949 — to commit forces against the world's dominant military power was presented by Mao Zedong's leadership as both a strategic necessity and an act of socialist solidarity. When UN forces under MacArthur crossed the 38th parallel in October 1950 and advanced toward the Yalu River on the Sino-Korean border, Beijing judged that its territorial security was directly threatened. On October 19, 1950, General Peng Dehuai led approximately 300,000 Chinese People's Volunteer Army (PVA) troops across the Yalu in a massive night offensive that shocked UN forces and reversed the trajectory of the war. The Chinese official narrative frames this intervention as a defensive response to imperialist aggression — a protective act to shield a fraternal socialist neighbor while securing China's northeastern border. Over the course of the conflict, more than 1.8 million PVA personnel served; official Chinese figures acknowledge approximately 180,000 dead, though independent estimates suggest significantly higher casualties.

The War to Resist America and Aid Korea holds a foundational place in the political mythology of the People's Republic. It is narrated as the moment when the "new China" demonstrated that it could stand up to and hold its own against the world's greatest power — the embodiment of Mao's declaration that "the Chinese people have stood up." The soldiers of the PVA were lionized in the phrase coined by war correspondent Wei Wei: they were "the most beloved people" (最可爱的人). The Xi Jinping administration staged major commemorations for the 70th anniversary in 2020, explicitly reframing the war in the context of U.S.-China strategic competition and presenting it as proof that China will resist external pressure regardless of cost. In official propaganda, the war demonstrates the superiority of "patriotic spirit" over material advantage — a narrative that has grown more prominent as China's relationship with the United States has deteriorated.

China's contemporary North Korea policy navigates the tension between its historical alliance relationship with the DPRK and its stated commitments to non-proliferation and regional stability. China accounts for more than 90 percent of North Korea's foreign trade and is by far the DPRK's most important economic lifeline. Beijing has formally supported "denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula" and advocated for dialogue frameworks including the Six-Party Talks, but has consistently resisted coercive measures it believes might destabilize the Kim regime. The fundamental Chinese strategic rationale is the concept of the "buffer state": a North Korean collapse would bring U.S.-allied forces to China's northeastern border, an outcome Beijing views as unacceptable regardless of North Korea's nuclear behavior. Since 2022, China has joined Russia in vetoing proposed UN Security Council measures against North Korea, effectively providing diplomatic cover for Pyongyang's continued missile testing and effectively fracturing the international sanctions architecture.

On the question of Korean unification and the maritime boundary disputes — including the disputed Northern Limit Line (NLL) established by UN Command in 1953 — China's position is officially one of non-interference while functionally preferring a stable status quo. A unified Korea allied with the United States is the scenario Beijing most wishes to avoid. China maintains diplomatic and economic relations with both Koreas but has vastly closer ties with Pyongyang on security matters. The Chinese government's insistence on resolving the Korean question through "dialogue, not pressure" and its resistance to American-led coercive strategies places it in fundamental opposition to the U.S.-South Korea alliance approach. This structural divergence — between Washington's condition of verified denuclearization before diplomatic progress and Beijing's preference for incremental confidence-building without preconditions — is perhaps the central reason the Korean nuclear impasse has proven intractable for three decades.
