When the Korean War broke out in June 1950, Japan was still under Allied occupation — a disarmed nation rebuilding under a constitution that renounced war. Yet geographically and strategically, Japan became indispensable as a rear base for the UN Command. U.S. forces launched operations from Japanese soil, and ports at Yokohama, Sasebo, and Okinawa served as critical logistical hubs. More consequentially for Japan's domestic trajectory was the phenomenon of "Korean War special procurements" (Chōsen tokuji). American military orders for materiel — vehicles, ammunition, textiles, steel — injected an estimated one billion dollars (in period value) into the Japanese economy between 1950 and 1953. Industries that had been languishing in postwar depression roared back to life, and the special procurement boom is widely credited as the economic catalyst that launched Japan's subsequent high-growth miracle. Japan's relationship with the Korean War is thus marked by an uncomfortable asymmetry: a neighboring tragedy that accelerated Japan's own recovery.

The Korean War also fundamentally reordered Japan's security architecture. General MacArthur, alarmed by the conflict, pressed the Japanese government to rearm, leading to the creation of the National Police Reserve (predecessor to the Self-Defense Forces) in August 1950. In September 1951, Japan signed the San Francisco Peace Treaty — restoring sovereignty — and simultaneously concluded a security treaty with the United States. This "San Francisco System" bound Japan into the U.S. strategic orbit and created the framework of bases, alliances, and defense commitments that persists to this day. The Self-Defense Forces, the U.S.-Japan alliance, and the permanent presence of American military installations in Japan are in significant measure products of the Korean War's geopolitical legacy. Japan's current government considers North Korea's nuclear and ballistic missile capabilities one of the most acute security threats it faces, and has consistently supported trilateral Japan-U.S.-South Korea security cooperation as the cornerstone of peninsula stability.

The legacy of Japan's colonial rule over Korea (1910–1945) creates a layer of historical complexity that continues to complicate Japan's engagement with the peninsula. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans came to Japan during the colonial period — some voluntarily, many through labor mobilization — and those who remained after 1945 and their descendants became the Zainichi Korean community, today numbering approximately 500,000. The Korean War and subsequent division split this community along ideological lines, with the Korean Residents Union (Mindan) aligned with South Korea and the General Association of Korean Residents (Chongryon) with the North. Japan's postwar legal framework long denied Zainichi Koreans a clear path to citizenship and subjected them to discriminatory treatment in employment, social services, and civic participation — a legacy of the colonial and Cold War periods that continues to generate legal and social debate. The abduction of Japanese nationals by North Korean agents — carried out primarily in the late 1970s and early 1980s — has profoundly shaped Japanese public opinion toward the DPRK and remains an unresolved precondition for any normalization of Japan-North Korea relations.

Japan's diplomatic approach to the Korean Peninsula is thus conditioned by multiple overlapping obligations and grievances. On the inter-Korean dimension, Japan officially supports peaceful reunification under democratic principles and engages actively in multilateral frameworks including the UN Security Council sanctions regime against North Korea. Simultaneously, unresolved historical disputes between Japan and South Korea — over wartime labor mobilization, the "comfort women" issue, and the sovereignty of the Takeshima/Dokdo islets — periodically disrupt the bilateral cooperation that regional security requires. Japan's position holds that comprehensive denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula is a prerequisite for any relaxation of pressure on Pyongyang, and that the abduction issue must be resolved before Japan-DPRK normalization can proceed. In navigating these intersecting demands, Japan occupies a uniquely conflicted position: historically entangled with both Koreas, strategically dependent on U.S. alliance management, and domestically motivated by issues — the abductions, Zainichi rights — that have no direct equivalent in other observer states.
