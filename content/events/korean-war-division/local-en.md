On the morning of June 25, 1950, North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel in a surprise invasion of the South. For South Koreans, this assault is the foundational trauma of the modern era — an unprovoked attack that drove nearly the entire peninsula under communist occupation within weeks. Seoul fell in three days; the Pusan Perimeter barely held. Only the intervention of United Nations forces, overwhelmingly American, and the subsequent Inchon landing under General Douglas MacArthur reversed the tide. The entry of Chinese People's Volunteer Forces in October 1950 pushed the front back to roughly the 38th parallel, where it stalemated into a brutal attritional conflict. The Armistice Agreement of July 27, 1953 ended the fighting but not the war — there is no peace treaty. Legally, the Korean War remains unresolved, and the peninsula exists in a state of frozen conflict sustained by the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), one of the most heavily fortified borders on earth.

The human cost of division is perhaps most viscerally represented by the approximately ten million Koreans separated from family members across the border. These divided families — parents and children, siblings, spouses — have been unable to communicate for more than seven decades. Reunion programs facilitated by the Red Cross and the two governments have been held intermittently since 1985 and more substantially since the 2000 inter-Korean summit, but political crises repeatedly interrupt them. As of 2018, over 130,000 South Koreans had registered with the government's divided family database, yet more than 70 percent of registrants had already died without reunion. The demographic clock is working against any resolution; each year, thousands more die waiting. For surviving separated family members — now mostly in their eighties and nineties — the division is not a political abstraction but a daily personal grief.

Civilian massacres and the accountability gap remain deeply contested terrain in local memory. The No Gun Ri massacre of July 1950, in which U.S. troops killed an estimated 250–300 South Korean refugees, was suppressed for decades and only entered public consciousness after a 1999 Associated Press investigation, which prompted a U.S. government acknowledgment of regret. But No Gun Ri was not isolated. South Korean Truth and Reconciliation Commission investigations have documented multiple incidents in which South Korean military and police summarily executed suspected communist sympathizers — most notoriously in the Bodo League massacres of 1950, where anywhere from tens of thousands to over 100,000 political prisoners were killed. North Korean forces similarly committed atrocities against civilians in occupied southern territory. The political suppression of these histories under successive South Korean military governments meant that full reckoning only began in the democratic era, and the north-side accounting remains entirely inaccessible.

Today, attitudes toward reunification in South Korea are undergoing a generational shift. Older Koreans who lived through the war or were directly affected by division often retain strong emotional commitments to reunification as a national imperative. But younger South Koreans increasingly view the North as a foreign-feeling entity rather than a sundered kin-state, and surveys consistently show declining enthusiasm for unification, driven by concerns about the enormous economic costs (estimates range into the trillions of dollars), the cultural and systemic divergence of 70-plus years, and the security risks posed by the North's nuclear arsenal. Civil society organizations continue to advocate for humanitarian engagement — food aid, family reunions, cultural exchange — as a foundation for eventual reconciliation. For ordinary Koreans on both sides of the DMZ, the war and division are not history; they are ongoing conditions that shape daily politics, family memory, and the existential question of what it means to be Korean.
