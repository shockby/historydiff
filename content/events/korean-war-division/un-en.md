The Korean War holds a singular place in the history of the United Nations as the first — and arguably still the most consequential — invocation of collective security under the UN Charter. Following North Korea's invasion on June 25, 1950, the Security Council passed Resolution 82 condemning the attack and Resolution 83 on June 27 recommending that member states furnish military assistance to South Korea. The passage of these resolutions was enabled by a historical contingency: the Soviet Union was boycotting the Council over the exclusion of the People's Republic of China and was therefore absent when the votes were taken. Sixteen nations contributed combat forces, and 41 more provided material or medical support under the UN flag. This collective response demonstrated that the Charter's collective security architecture could function — but the subsequent military stalemate following China's intervention also exposed its limits in contexts where major-power interests directly collided.

More than seven decades after the July 27, 1953 Armistice Agreement, the Korean Peninsula remains legally in a state of war. The UN Command (UNC), now comprising representatives of 17 member states, continues to oversee the terms of the armistice and manage the Military Demarcation Line and DMZ. No peace treaty has ever been concluded between the belligerents, making the Korean conflict the world's longest unresolved formal armed conflict. The UN General Assembly passes annual resolutions on the human rights situation in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK), and the 2014 Commission of Inquiry (COI) — established by the Human Rights Council — produced a landmark report documenting what it characterized as crimes against humanity committed by the DPRK authorities: systematic use of political prison camps, enforced disappearances, deliberate starvation, and other grave abuses. The DPRK rejected the report entirely as politically motivated interference in its internal affairs.

North Korea's nuclear and ballistic missile programs have become the central preoccupation of the Security Council's engagement with the peninsula. Since the DPRK's first nuclear test in 2006, the Council adopted a series of increasingly comprehensive sanctions regimes — Resolutions 1718, 1874, 2094, 2270, 2321, 2371, 2375, and 2397 — targeting coal, iron ore, seafood exports, petroleum imports, and overseas labor. However, the sanctions architecture has progressively fractured. Since 2022, China and Russia have used their veto power to block additional measures, reflecting a broader realignment in which the geopolitical interests of permanent Council members override the non-proliferation consensus. This dynamic mirrors the structural problem that confronted the Council in 1950: collective security mechanisms become inoperable when the major powers disagree.

The international humanitarian system faces its own contradictions regarding the DPRK. UN agencies including the World Food Programme, UNICEF, and the World Health Organization have sought to deliver humanitarian assistance to North Korea's chronically food-insecure population, but the DPRK government's severe restrictions on access, monitoring, and independent verification have made it nearly impossible to assess whether aid reaches the most vulnerable. The multilateral diplomatic framework represented by the Six-Party Talks (2003–2009) — involving the two Koreas, the United States, China, Japan, and Russia — has effectively collapsed as North Korea's nuclear capabilities have advanced well beyond what the talks aimed to prevent. For the international community, the Korean Peninsula represents a compounded challenge: an unresolved armed conflict, a severe human rights crisis, an active nuclear proliferation problem, and a humanitarian emergency, all intertwined and resistant to the standard toolkit of international dispute resolution.
