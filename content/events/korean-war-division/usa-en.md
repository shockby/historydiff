The American entry into the Korean War on June 27, 1950 was framed from the outset as a response to naked communist aggression against a fledgling democracy and a test of the credibility of the new United Nations collective security system. President Harry Truman, acting under the authority of the UN Security Council resolution and without a formal congressional declaration of war (a precedent-setting decision he labeled a "police action"), committed U.S. forces within 48 hours of the North Korean invasion. The strategic logic was rooted in the Truman Doctrine and NSC-68: appeasement of communist expansion anywhere risked emboldening it everywhere. The spectacular success of General MacArthur's Inchon landing in September 1950 briefly appeared to offer total victory — driving North Korean forces back to the Yalu River — before China's massive intervention in October transformed the conflict into an attritional slog. MacArthur's public advocacy for expanding the war, including potential use of nuclear weapons against China, led Truman to relieve him of command in April 1951, a civil-military crisis that underscored the constitutional primacy of civilian control.

The armistice of July 1953 — negotiated through two years of grinding talks at Panmunjom while fighting continued — left the United States in a strategically ambiguous position. The war ended not in victory but in stalemate at roughly the prewar line of demarcation. American public memory of Korea as "The Forgotten War" — sandwiched between the moral clarity of World War II and the cultural upheaval of Vietnam — reflects a national discomfort with limited wars fought to inconclusive draws. Nevertheless, the U.S. commitment to South Korea deepened rather than diminished after 1953. The 1953 U.S.-Republic of Korea Mutual Defense Treaty established a formal alliance, and U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) — currently approximately 28,500 troops — have been stationed on the peninsula continuously. American strategic doctrine holds that a credible combined defense posture, including the "nuclear umbrella" extended deterrence commitment, is essential to deterring North Korean aggression and reassuring South Korea and Japan.

North Korea's nuclear weapons program has represented the central challenge to U.S. Korea policy since the early 1990s. Washington has pursued this issue through successive administrations using varying combinations of pressure and diplomacy. The 1994 Agreed Framework under President Clinton temporarily froze Pyongyang's plutonium program in exchange for energy assistance and a pathway to normalization, but the agreement collapsed in 2002 over allegations of a clandestine uranium enrichment program. The Bush administration's policy of refusing bilateral negotiation without multilateral framework led to the Six-Party Talks (2003–2009), which achieved a brief agreement in 2005 but failed to produce verified denuclearization. The dramatic summit diplomacy of the Trump administration — three meetings between President Trump and Kim Jong Un in 2018–2019, including the historic Singapore summit — generated no substantive arms control progress. The Biden administration reverted to demanding preconditions before high-level engagement, and the DPRK did not respond to diplomatic outreach. Through all these shifts, North Korea has continued to advance its nuclear and missile capabilities, conducting six nuclear tests between 2006 and 2017 and developing intercontinental ballistic missiles capable of reaching the continental United States.

The United States also maintains a significant stake in the unresolved POW/MIA issue. Approximately 7,500 American service members remain unaccounted for from the Korean War — a number that represents the largest remaining MIA caseload from any American conflict. Remains recovery operations, conducted in cooperation with North Korea in the late 1990s and intermittently thereafter, have provided partial accounting but were suspended after relations deteriorated. For the U.S. government, the Korean question today encompasses deterrence of North Korean aggression, prevention of nuclear and missile technology proliferation, enforcement of the UN sanctions regime, humanitarian advocacy regarding the DPRK's human rights record, and alliance management with South Korea and Japan — a complex of interests that successive administrations have struggled to balance coherently in the face of an adversary that has proved adept at exploiting inter-alliance differences and resisting international pressure.
