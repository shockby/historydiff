For Kosovo Albanians, who constitute roughly 90 percent of Kosovo's population, the 1990s represented a decade of systematic dispossession under Serbian President Slobodan Milošević. Following the unilateral revocation of Kosovo's substantial autonomy in 1989, Albanian-language education and public administration were abolished, and tens of thousands of Albanian civil servants were summarily dismissed. The parallel society that emerged — with its own shadow schools, clinics, and tax system organized around Ibrahim Rugova's Democratic League of Kosovo (LDK) — sustained a policy of non-violent resistance for nearly a decade. When the Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA/UÇK) escalated its armed campaign after 1996, many Kosovo Albanians saw it not as terrorism but as an inevitable response to the failure of peaceful means to attract meaningful international intervention. The KLA's designation as a terrorist organization by the United States in 1998 was later effectively reversed as the U.S. engaged directly with KLA leadership during the Rambouillet negotiations.

The 1998–1999 war brought organized violence against civilians on a scale that transformed international opinion. The Račak massacre of January 1999, in which Serbian security forces killed at least 45 Albanian civilians, provided the immediate catalyst for NATO's ultimatum and, ultimately, its 78-day air campaign beginning March 24, 1999. For Kosovo Albanians, Operation Allied Force is understood not as an illegal act of aggression but as a life-saving humanitarian intervention that halted ethnic cleansing operations which had already displaced hundreds of thousands of people. The June 1999 withdrawal of Yugoslav forces, the deployment of KFOR, and the establishment of UNMIK marked the end of Serbian administrative control and the beginning of internationally supervised self-governance. These events are commemorated annually and remain the foundational narrative of the contemporary Kosovo state.

The February 17, 2008 declaration of independence — read to a jubilant Pristina crowd by Prime Minister Hashim Thaçi — represented the culmination of nearly a decade of supervised independence preparation under UN and EU auspices. Grounded in the Ahtisaari Plan (formally the Comprehensive Proposal for the Kosovo Status Process), the declaration included explicit protections for minority communities, cultural heritage sites, and decentralized governance structures. The 2010 ICJ Advisory Opinion, which found that the declaration did not violate international law, was celebrated across Kosovo as definitive international legal validation. Today, with over 100 UN member states recognizing Kosovo's statehood and the country holding membership in the IMF, World Bank, and Council of Europe, Kosovo Albanians increasingly frame their national identity around Euro-Atlantic integration rather than the conflict itself.

Kosovo Serbs, concentrated primarily in the northern municipalities above the Ibar River and in scattered enclaves across central and southern Kosovo, inhabit a starkly different political reality. Following KFOR's deployment in 1999, an estimated 100,000–200,000 Serbs and other non-Albanian minorities fled Kosovo, largely due to retaliatory violence and insecurity. The March 2004 riots — in which mobs burned over 900 Serbian homes and churches and killed 19 people — exposed the continued vulnerability of remaining Serb communities even under international supervision. For Kosovo Serbs, Kosovo is not a post-conflict success story but an ongoing displacement crisis in which their constitutional rights within the Serbian state have been severed by an internationally endorsed act of secession. The north, centered on Mitrovica, operates largely parallel to Pristina's institutions, with Serbian-language schools, Serbian dinar currency, and political allegiance to Belgrade. The 2022–2023 barricade crises, triggered by disputes over vehicle license plates and the arrest of a former Kosovo Serb police officer, illustrated how deeply the political settlement remains contested on the ground.
