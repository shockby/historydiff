The Kurds are the world's largest stateless nation, numbering between 35 and 40 million people spread across Turkey, Iraq, Syria, and Iran — a historically Kurdish region known as Kurdistan. Despite forming a distinct ethnic, linguistic, and cultural community, they have never achieved recognized statehood.

The 1920 Treaty of Sèvres promised a Kurdish homeland following the collapse of the Ottoman Empire, but the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne reversed that commitment. Since then, Kurdish communities have faced systematic suppression of their language, culture, and political aspirations in all four states.

In Turkey, the Kurdish language was banned for decades and Kurds were officially labeled "Mountain Turks." In Iraq, Saddam Hussein's 1988 Anfal campaign and the chemical weapons attack on Halabja — killing approximately 5,000 civilians — were recognized internationally as genocide. In Syria, Kurdish fighters (YPG/SDF) were instrumental in defeating ISIS yet were subsequently sidelined from political negotiations.

The Kurdish aspiration is either an independent state or meaningful autonomy and cultural recognition within existing borders. The PKK's armed campaign against Turkey is rooted, from the Kurdish perspective, in decades of state violence and denial of basic rights. Kurds argue that sustained peace requires genuine political inclusion, not military suppression alone.
