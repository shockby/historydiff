Western engagement with the Kurdish question has been shaped by a persistent tension between strategic interests and democratic values, producing a foreign policy record that Kurdish advocates often describe as one of repeated betrayal.

During the Cold War, U.S. support for NATO ally Turkey meant that Kurdish rights were largely ignored. After the 1991 Gulf War, the U.S. and UK enforced no-fly zones to protect Iraqi Kurds from Saddam Hussein while stopping well short of supporting independence. Following the 2003 Iraq War, U.S. forces forged a close partnership with the Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP) and Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK), hailing the Kurdistan Region of Iraq as a stable, pro-Western enclave.

In Syria, the YPG-led Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) became the primary ground force defeating ISIS, receiving U.S. arms and training over strenuous Turkish objections. In October 2019, President Trump's abrupt withdrawal of U.S. troops ahead of Turkey's Operation Peace Spring drew bipartisan condemnation in Congress and international criticism, with many arguing America had abandoned its most effective anti-ISIS partner.

In Europe, large Kurdish diaspora communities exert political influence. European parliaments have repeatedly passed resolutions criticizing Turkish military operations and calling for minority rights protections. The EU accession process had historically encouraged Turkey toward political reforms affecting Kurds, though that leverage has waned significantly since the mid-2010s.
