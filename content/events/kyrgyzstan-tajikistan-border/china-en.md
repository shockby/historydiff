China views the Kyrgyzstan-Tajikistan border conflict primarily through the lens of Central Asian regional stability. Both countries are members of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO), and China has consistently called for peaceful resolution through dialogue within the SCO framework. During the 2021 and 2022 clashes, China issued official statements calling for ceasefires and emphasizing solidarity and dialogue among SCO member states. Beijing has carefully avoided taking sides, maintaining its principles of non-interference in internal affairs and resolution through negotiation. China's role as the SCO's founding power and largest economy gives it implicit influence over the process without requiring active mediation.

The most critical dimension of this conflict for China is the nexus between Central Asian stability, Xinjiang security, and Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) connectivity. The Fergana Valley is geographically proximate to China's western Xinjiang region, and cross-border instability carries potential implications for China's governance challenges in Xinjiang. China has invested substantially in infrastructure and energy projects in both Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan — roads, hydropower facilities, and special economic zones — as part of securing BRI logistics routes connecting China to Central Asia, South Asia, and onward. Regional destabilization directly threatens these economic interests. China has also provided both countries with debt financing, creating bilateral financial dependencies that reinforce Chinese influence.

Water resource issues represent another dimension of Chinese interest. China monitors the upstream-downstream dynamics of Central Asian water management, including Tajikistan's major dam projects (Nurek, Rogun) and their implications for regional water allocation. As a major upstream riparian power itself on multiple Asian river systems (the Mekong, Brahmaputra, Irrawaddy), China observes Central Asian water disputes with a degree of professional and strategic attention. On the question of border demarcation, China has concluded bilateral border treaties with both Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan, and the Chinese-Kyrgyz and Chinese-Tajik frontiers are comparatively stable — a contrast that implicitly positions China as a model of bilateral border management while the two countries' mutual boundary remains contested.
