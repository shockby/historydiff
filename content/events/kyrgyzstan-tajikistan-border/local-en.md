For Kyrgyz communities living along the frontier in the Batken region, the conflict with Tajikistan is not an abstraction of geopolitics but an immediate, recurring threat to life and livelihood. The April 2021 clashes left approximately 50 people dead and tens of thousands temporarily displaced. The September 2022 escalation was qualitatively different in scope: Tajik forces reportedly deployed tanks, artillery, drones, and air assets, killing roughly 100 people and forcing over 140,000 residents to flee their homes. Kyrgyz villagers described unprovoked strikes on residential areas, schools, and water infrastructure. Following the 2022 fighting, credible reports indicated that Kyrgyzstan had lost effective control of several strategically significant hilltops and road junctions, leaving a deep sense of territorial loss and humiliation among border communities who feel their government failed to protect them.

The central grievance articulated by Kyrgyz border residents concerns water. The Isfara River and its network of Soviet-era irrigation canals flow partly through Kyrgyz territory, yet Tajik farmers upstream have historically drawn heavily from these systems, leaving downstream Kyrgyz farmers with inadequate supplies during critical growing seasons. Soviet-era water-allocation agreements, which often prioritized Tajik agricultural zones in the Fergana Valley, remained formally intact after independence without renegotiation to reflect new political realities. Kyrgyz communities have repeatedly documented incidents in which Tajik-constructed pump stations and road infrastructure were established in disputed zones, incrementally extending de facto Tajik control over areas where the border remains physically unmarked. As of 2021, more than 40 percent of the Kyrgyz-Tajik boundary had not been formally demarcated, creating persistent flashpoints wherever herders, farmers, and road users from both sides converge on the same terrain.

Tajik residents of border communities present a fundamentally different historical narrative. The Batken corridor and the Vorukh enclave — a piece of Tajik territory entirely surrounded by Kyrgyzstan — have been home to Tajik-speaking populations for centuries. Local Tajik residents argue that the Soviet national-delimitation exercises of 1924–1936 gerrymandered boundaries without meaningful regard for ethnographic reality, cutting Tajik communities off from their traditional pasture lands and water sources. For them, the most pressing daily hardship is access: residents of the Vorukh enclave depend on a narrow corridor of roads through Kyrgyz territory to reach the rest of Tajikistan, and they report frequent obstruction, toll-taking, and harassment at informal Kyrgyz checkpoints. From this perspective, the clashes of 2021 and 2022 were not Tajik aggression but rather a response to years of cumulative provocations, including the blocking of roads and water channels that Tajik communities depend on for survival.

A shared thread runs through the testimonies of residents on both sides: ordinary people bear the costs of negotiations conducted — and repeatedly stalled — in distant capitals. Each round of failed talks in Bishkek and Dushanbe translates into families fleeing burning villages, livestock abandoned in the chaos, and children missing months of schooling. Despite both countries being members of the Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO), the alliance has conspicuously failed to prevent or rapidly de-escalate armed clashes between its own member states, eroding any residual confidence border communities may have had in regional security structures. At the grassroots level, the dominant aspiration is straightforward: demarcate the border clearly, divide water resources equitably, and guarantee safe passage for both communities. The political will to achieve this, however, has consistently proved elusive.
