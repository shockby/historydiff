For the Arab world — particularly Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states — the Middle East's security landscape is defined by a persistent tension between Islamic solidarity and geopolitical realism. While officially championing the Palestinian cause, these states view **Iran's bid for regional hegemony** as their most immediate existential threat. As the self-styled guardian of Sunni Islam, Saudi Arabia regards Iran's Shia proxy network — Hezbollah in Lebanon, the Houthis in Yemen, and Iran-backed militias in Iraq — as a direct challenge to regional stability and national survival.

The **Yemeni Civil War** (from 2014 onward) brought this threat into sharp relief. A Saudi-led Arab coalition engaged the Houthis directly, as the Iran-backed group launched repeated missile and drone strikes into Saudi territory. The conflict demonstrated, in concrete military terms, the reach of Iran's "arc of influence" strategy. Yet the prolonged war also drew international criticism over its devastating humanitarian toll, creating a diplomatic burden for Riyadh.

The **Abraham Accords of 2020** — normalizing relations between Israel and the UAE, Bahrain, Sudan, and Morocco — must be understood in this strategic context. Critics across the Arab world condemned the agreements as abandoning the Palestinian cause. From the Gulf states' perspective, however, the Accords represented pragmatic security cooperation against a shared Iranian threat and a means to deepen the American security umbrella. The move illustrated the widening gap between official Arab rhetoric on Palestine and the actual foreign policy calculus of Gulf governments.

**China's brokering of Saudi-Iranian rapprochement in March 2023** surprised global observers and signaled Riyadh's growing interest in diversifying its diplomatic partnerships beyond Washington. Yet the underlying ideological rivalry and proxy conflicts were not resolved overnight. Most analysts see the normalization as fragile, with structural tensions intact beneath the diplomatic surface.

The **Gaza conflict of 2023** placed Gulf governments in an awkward position. Publicly, Arab states voiced strong support for Palestinians. Internally, however, concerns ran deep: Hamas is backed by Iran, and an escalating Gaza crisis threatened to derail the region's ambitious modernization agendas — most notably Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030. In the energy domain, Gulf states continue to use their influence within OPEC+ to manage relationships with Western powers while asserting a degree of strategic autonomy on Middle East policy.
