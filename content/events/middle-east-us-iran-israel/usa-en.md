For the United States, Middle East policy has rested on three pillars: ensuring the security of its democratic ally Israel, securing access to energy resources, and preventing regional hegemony by "rogue states" such as the Soviet Union, Russia, and Iran.

**President Truman** recognized the newly established State of Israel just eleven minutes after its declaration of independence in 1948, and the United States has remained Israel's "ironclad" partner ever since. The **1953 ousting of Mosaddegh** has been contextualized as a Cold War necessity to block Soviet southward expansion and protect oil supplies — though it has drawn persistent criticism on human rights grounds.

The **1979 hostage crisis (444 days)** destabilized the Carter administration and became the immediate cause of the severing of U.S.-Iran relations. The **Reagan administration** simultaneously supported Iraq during the Iran-Iraq War while becoming embroiled in the Iran-Contra affair — covertly selling arms to Iran — a contradictory foreign policy that reflected the complexity of regional dynamics. The **1991 Gulf War** and the **2003 Iraq War** decisively expanded America's military presence across the Middle East.

The **Obama administration's JCPOA (2015)** was hailed as the "best available deal" to diplomatically constrain Iran's nuclear program. The **Trump administration** withdrew in 2018, branding it the "worst deal ever," and adopted a maximum-pressure strategy. The assassination of IRGC Commander Soleimani in 2020 was justified as the elimination of a "terrorist" who directed Iran's regional proxy network. The **Biden administration** sought to return to diplomacy, but negotiations stalled as Iran advanced its nuclear activities. In **June 2025**, the decision to conduct precision strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities — in a joint operation with Israel — was driven by the imperative to enforce the "red line" against a nuclear-armed Iran.
