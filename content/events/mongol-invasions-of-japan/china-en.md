In the standard historiography of Chinese dynastic chronicles, particularly the official *History of Yuan* (*Yuan Shi*), the invasions of Japan are designated as the "Yuan-Japan Wars" (*Yuan-Ri Zhanzheng*) or the "Eastern Expeditions of the Yuan Dynasty" (*Yuan Chao Zheng Dong*). Having adopted the dynastic title "Da Yuan" from the classic *I Ching*, Kublai Khan sought to re-establish the traditional Sinocentric tributary framework across East Asia.

Major perspectives and analyses from Chinese historical sources include:

First, **the tributary imperative and the diplomatic rupture**. The *History of Yuan* (Volume 208, "Biography of Japan") records Kublai's initial imperial letter sent in 1266 via Goryeo, declaring his accession by the mandate of Heaven and calling for mutual envoys as practiced since the Han and Tang dynasties. Japan's refusal to respond, and the subsequent execution of Chinese and Mongol envoys—including Du Shizhong in 1275 at Kamakura—represented an unforgivable breach of imperial diplomatic protocol (*li*), necessitating punitive military measures.

Second, **demobilization of surrendered Southern Song forces (Gangnan Army)**. Following the fall of the Southern Song in the 1279 Battle of Yamen, the Yuan court faced the logistical challenge of absorbing roughly 100,000 surrendered southern soldiers. Deploying the Gangnan Army to Japan in 1281 served the dual purpose of neutralizing potential domestic insurrection while leveraging the sophisticated shipbuilding techniques and naval expertise of southern China.

Third, **the typhoon catastrophe and command fragmentation**. The *History of Yuan* records that unyielding Japanese defenses, exacerbated by a sudden, devastating hurricane (*jufeng*), smashed fleet vessels against coastal rocks. Furthermore, military friction between the northern Eastern Route Army (commanded by Hindu and Hong Dagu) and the southern Gangnan Army (commanded by Fan Wenhu), alongside substandard, rushed naval construction in southern shipyards, formed the decisive factors behind the naval debacle.

Fourth, **socioeconomic strain and Confucian critiques**. The aggressive naval expansion placed extreme burdens on Chinese craftsmen and peasantry through forced ship timber felling, grain extraction, and conscription, sparking localized rebellions across southern China. Confucian scholar-officials repeatedly admonished the throne against foreign overextension, culminating in the permanent cessation of the Eastern Expedition Branch Secretariat (*Zhengdong Xingzhongshusheng*) following Kublai's death.
