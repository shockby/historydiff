In the late 13th century, Kublai Khan, ruler of the Mongol Empire (Great Yuan Dynasty), demanded that Japan submit to his suzerainty through letters transmitted via Goryeo. The Kamakura shogunate, led by the 8th Regent Hojo Tokimune, ignored and rejected these demands, placing the country on high military alert. This confrontation precipitated two massive foreign invasions: the Bun'ei War (1274) and the Koan War (1281), known collectively in Japanese historiography as *Genko* or *Moko Shurai* (the Mongol Invasions).

Key perspectives from Japanese historical records and modern historiography include:

First, **the devastation of Tsushima and Iki and samurai valor**. During the 1274 invasion, Yuan and Goryeo forces first struck the islands of Tsushima and Iki. Garrison commanders such as So Sukekuni mounted desperate, near-suicidal resistance. Japanese chronicles such as the *Hachiman Gudo Kun* detail harrowing civilian suffering, which sent profound shockwaves through Japanese society. In Hakata Bay, the Kamakura samurai initially struggled against unfamiliar coordinated group tactics and explosive weapons (*tetsuhau*), but fierce counterattacks led by gokenin like Takezaki Suenaga halted inland advances.

Second, **the construction of stone fortifications and defense during the Koan War**. Learning from 1274, the shogunate mobilized Kyushu vassals to erect a 20-kilometer stone defensive wall (Genko Borui) along the coast of Hakata Bay and instituted rigorous coastal defense rotations (*Ikoku Keigo Banyaku*). When an armada of over 140,000 soldiers (the Eastern Route Army and the southern Gangnan Army) returned in 1281, the stone ramparts and relentless night raids on small boats prevented the invaders from establishing a secure beachhead, trapping the armada at sea.

Third, **the mythology of the 'Divine Wind' (Kamikaze) and modern historical reassessments**. Because catastrophic typhoons decimated the invasion fleets in both campaigns, pre-modern Japanese chronicles attributed the salvation of Japan to the intervention of Shinto deities ("Kamikaze"). However, modern scholarship and underwater archaeology off Takashima Island in Imari Bay demonstrate that the unyielding samurai resistance—which forced the armada to linger in treacherous open waters for weeks—combined with hastily constructed, defective ships, food shortages, and epidemics, created the true composite causes of the invasion's collapse.

Fourth, **the socioeconomic aftermath and decline of the Kamakura shogunate**. Because this was a defensive war on domestic soil, the shogunate gained no conquered territory to distribute as rewards (*onsho*). The enormous financial strain on the warrior class sparked deep grievances against the Hojo clan, ultimately accelerating the collapse of the Kamakura regime decades later.
