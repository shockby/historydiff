In Korean historiography, the 13th-century military campaigns against Japan are known as the "Combined Goryeo-Mongol Expeditions to Japan" (*Yeo-Mong Yeonhapgun-ui Ilbon Wonjeong*). Having suffered nearly three decades of devastating Mongol invasions and internal revolts by the Sambyeolcho, Goryeo was reduced to a subordinate son-in-law kingdom (*fuma guo*) under the direct suzerainty of the Great Khan.

Key perspectives from Korean historical records and modern scholarship include:

First, **devastating national exhaustion under Mongol coercion**. Kublai Khan compelled King Wonjong and King Chungnyeol to supply warships, grain, and conscripts for the overseas campaigns. For the 1274 invasion, Goryeo was forced to construct over 900 military vessels in under four months. The *Goryeosa* repeatedly laments that "the people could not endure the misery," noting that widespread requisition of timber, agricultural disruption during planting seasons, and famine decimated the peasantry.

Second, **institutional encroachment through the Eastern Expedition Branch Secretariat**. To oversee the conquest of Japan, the Yuan court established the *Zhengdong Xingzhongshusheng* (Jeongdong Haengseong) in the Goryeo capital of Gaegyeong (Kaesong). Even after the expeditions failed, this administrative apparatus was retained, serving as an enduring mechanism for Mongol surveillance, interference in royal succession, and administrative control over Goryeo domestic politics.

Third, **the military ordeal and battlefield sacrifice of Goryeo commanders**. The *Biography of Kim Bang-gyeong* in the *Goryeosa* documents the valiant yet tragic efforts of the Goryeo commander Kim Bang-gyeong, who led frontline combat during both invasions. Korean sources highlight severe disagreements with Mongol commanders such as Hindu, as well as the heroic efforts to salvage sinking vessels amidst terrifying typhoons, resulting in the drowning of thousands of Goryeo soldiers.

Fourth, **the dual character of participant and victim**. Modern Korean history textbooks and academic scholarship acknowledge Goryeo's participation as an allied force while emphasizing that Goryeo acted under severe imperial coercion as a victim of Mongol expansionism. The catastrophic loss of lives and national wealth during these expeditions eroded Goryeo's institutional foundations, setting the stage for King Gongmin's later anti-Yuan reforms and the eventual rise of the Joseon Dynasty.
