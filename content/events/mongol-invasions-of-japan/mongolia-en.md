From the perspective of Mongolian and Eurasian nomadic imperial history, the 13th-century expeditions against the Japanese archipelago formed an integral part of the global expansion initiated by Genghis Khan, specifically the outward maritime projection of the Great Yuan Ulus established by Kublai Khan.

Key themes emphasized in Mongolian and steppe imperial historiography include:

First, **the Mandate of Tengri and universal world order**. Nomadic imperial statecraft held that Tengri (the Eternal Blue Sky) had bestowed upon the Chinggisid dynasty the sovereign mandate to govern all terrestrial realms under heaven. In the imperial missive dispatched to Japan, Kublai framed his overtures under universal fraternal harmony ("making all within the four seas one family"), stating that resort to arms was universally undesirable. Refusal to enter tributary and diplomatic relations was regarded by Karakorum and Dadu as a direct challenge to the rightful universal order.

Second, **the geopolitical blockade of the Southern Song and maritime control**. During the first invasion in 1274, Kublai Khan was engaged in a protracted war of attrition against the Southern Song Dynasty. Japan served as a critical trading partner and financial conduit for the Southern Song through maritime trade and Zen Buddhist exchanges. Subjugating or neutralizing Japan was a strategic priority to complete the economic encirclement of the Song. Following the Song's downfall in 1279, the 1281 invasion represented an ambition to dominate the maritime trade arteries of the East China Sea.

Third, **the evolution from steppe cavalry to a composite maritime empire**. The Mongol campaigns against Japan marked the empire's transition from landlocked steppe cavalry warfare into a combined-arms, multinational naval force incorporating northern Chinese, Goryeo, and southern Chinese naval forces, complete with advanced gunpowder armaments. This operational scale served as a precursor to subsequent maritime expeditions dispatched to Champa (southern Vietnam) and Java (Indonesia).

Fourth, **climatic catastrophe and the pragmatic curtailment of campaigns**. As recorded in Persian chronicles such as Rashid al-Din's *Jami al-Tawarikh*, catastrophic typhoons destroyed the fleets, causing severe material and human losses. Although Kublai contemplated a third invasion, internal rebellion by the House of Ogedei (Qaidu) in Central Asia, military commitments in Southeast Asia, and domestic burdens led him to permanently abandon the project. While the defeat marked the maritime boundary of Mongol expansion, it did not threaten the core foundations of the vast continental Yuan realm.
