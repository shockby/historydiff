Japan's engagement with the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict has been shaped by its absence from the OSCE Minsk Group, its limited geostrategic footprint in the South Caucasus, and its traditional preference for multilateral diplomacy over unilateral declaratory positions. Across the conflict's various phases, the Japanese Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MOFA) consistently issued calls for an immediate cessation of hostilities and a negotiated settlement in accordance with international law—particularly the principles of state sovereignty and territorial integrity. Japan maintained diplomatic relations with both Armenia and Azerbaijan throughout the conflict, and its official communications carefully avoided singling out either party for condemnation. This posture of calibrated neutrality reflected both Japan's genuine preference for dialogue-based solutions and the practical consideration that explicit alignment with either party could damage bilateral relationships without producing meaningful diplomatic leverage.

The 44-Day War of 2020 drew measured statements from Japanese officials, including then-Foreign Minister Taro Kono, who called for an immediate ceasefire without specifically addressing the roles of Turkey or Russia in the conflict. Japan's response to the September 2023 offensive was similarly restrained. While MOFA expressed concern about the humanitarian situation and the mass displacement of civilians, Japan stopped well short of the explicit condemnations of ethnic cleansing issued by some European governments and the United States. Diet deliberations on the crisis were limited; a small number of opposition politicians and civil society groups expressed solidarity with Armenians, citing historical echoes of the Armenian Genocide, but these voices did not generate sufficient political momentum to alter the government's official stance. The Armenian diaspora community in Japan is extremely small compared to those in France, the United States, or Russia, meaning that domestic political pressure from this constituency is virtually absent.

On the humanitarian front, Japan has provided modest but consistent contributions through multilateral channels. Following the 2023 exodus, the Japanese government channeled emergency humanitarian assistance funding through MOFA's grant aid mechanism to organizations including UNHCR, supporting shelter, food security, and medical care for displaced Armenians arriving in the Republic of Armenia. Japan's JICA has operated development cooperation programs in both Armenia and Azerbaijan over the years, focusing on economic governance, agricultural development, and capacity building for civil society. However, the scale of Japan's humanitarian engagement in this region remains small relative to European and American counterparts, and the South Caucasus does not feature prominently in Japan's strategic ODA priorities, which are oriented more toward Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Pacific.

Several structural factors explain Japan's limited engagement with the Nagorno-Karabakh question. Japan's own unresolved territorial dispute over the Northern Territories (Kuril Islands) with Russia creates political sensitivities around the self-determination versus territorial integrity debate; Tokyo tends to prefer framing such disputes through the lens of international law without endorsing secessionism as a general principle. Japan also has an energy security interest in maintaining stable relationships with Azerbaijan, a significant oil and gas producer, via the Southern Gas Corridor. More broadly, Japan's foreign policy culture prioritizes consensus-building within established multilateral frameworks—the UN, G7, and OECD—rather than independent bilateral pressure campaigns. Within these constraints, Japan's approach has been to affirm universal principles of international law and humanitarian concern while keeping its operational commitments proportionate to its actual influence in the region.
