For the ethnic Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh—known in Armenian as Artsakh—the region represents far more than contested territory. It is a homeland with documented continuous habitation stretching back over two millennia, studded with medieval monasteries, churches, and khachkars (stone crosses) that form the physical spine of Armenian cultural identity. When the Soviet Union began to fracture in 1988, the Armenian-majority population of the Nagorno-Karabakh Autonomous Oblast voted to unite with the Armenian Soviet Socialist Republic, triggering a cycle of pogroms and inter-communal violence on both sides. The First Nagorno-Karabakh War (1988–1994) ended with Armenian forces in de facto control of the enclave and seven surrounding Azerbaijani districts, a military outcome that gave rise to the unrecognized but functioning Republic of Artsakh. For Armenians, the 1994 ceasefire was not a conquest but a survival—an imperfect peace purchased in blood.

The 44-Day War of September–November 2020 shattered that precarious equilibrium. Armed with Turkish-supplied Bayraktar TB2 drones, Israeli loitering munitions, and Syrian mercenaries—an unprecedented combination in post-Soviet conflicts—Azerbaijani forces dismantled Armenian defensive lines with devastating efficiency. The Russian-brokered ceasefire of November 9, 2020 forced Armenia to cede Hadrut, Shusha (Shushi), and large swaths of territory. For the Armenian community, the loss of Shusha—the cultural capital where Komitas Vardapet studied and where Armenian composers and poets shaped modern national culture—was a wound of exceptional depth. Russian peacekeepers deployed under the ceasefire agreement were expected to guarantee the security of remaining Armenians in Nagorno-Karabakh; that expectation would prove tragically misplaced.

The Azerbaijani offensive of September 19, 2023, labeled by Baku as an "anti-terrorist operation," lasted fewer than 24 hours before Artsakh's defense forces capitulated. The speed and totality of the collapse left the civilian population of approximately 120,000 with almost no time to consider alternatives. Within days, an estimated 100,000 to 120,000 ethnic Armenians—virtually the entire remaining population—fled across the Lachin corridor into Armenia. Eyewitness accounts collected by Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and investigative journalists described chaotic, traumatic evacuations; families abandoning livestock, documents, and heirlooms under reported threats and fear of retribution. The Republic of Artsakh formally dissolved on October 1, 2023. The international human rights community has widely characterized the demographic outcome as ethnic cleansing, while the ICJ had already found plausible evidence of violations under the Genocide Convention. The failure of Russian peacekeepers to intervene—despite their explicit mandate—left Armenians feeling abandoned by the one guarantor they had been compelled to trust.

The experience of Azerbaijani internally displaced persons (IDPs) and their descendants presents an essential, often overlooked counterweight. Between 1988 and 1994, an estimated 600,000 to 800,000 ethnic Azerbaijanis were expelled from Armenian-controlled territories across Karabakh and the surrounding districts—places like Aghdam, Fuzuli, Jabrayil, and Kelbajar—in one of the largest forced displacements in post-Soviet history. For thirty years, these communities lived in temporary housing across Azerbaijan, raising children on memories of homes they could not return to. For them, the 2020 and 2023 military operations represented not aggression but liberation—the restoration of rights denied by what they experienced as illegal Armenian occupation. The Azerbaijani government has launched ambitious reconstruction projects in "liberated territories," including the planned new city of Aghali, and IDPs have begun returning to reclaim ancestral land. The human reality on both sides—displacement, loss, longing, and the desperate desire for a homeland—reveals the profound tragedy of a conflict where competing legitimate claims could not be reconciled through diplomacy alone.
