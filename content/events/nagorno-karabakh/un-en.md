The United Nations Security Council engaged with the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict at an early stage, adopting four resolutions during the height of the First War in 1993. Resolutions 822, 853, 874, and 884 each demanded the immediate withdrawal of occupying forces from the internationally recognized territory of Azerbaijan, affirming Azerbaijani territorial integrity as a matter of international law. The resolutions called for cessation of hostilities, unimpeded access for humanitarian assistance, and the resumption of negotiations under the auspices of the OSCE. However, none of these resolutions contained binding enforcement mechanisms under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, and the complex web of great-power interests—Russia's alliance with Armenia, Western dependence on Azerbaijani energy exports, and Turkey's growing regional ambitions—precluded any coercive follow-through. The result was a pattern that would persist for three decades: international legal declarations without operational consequence.

The OSCE Minsk Group, established in 1992 with France, Russia, and the United States as co-chairs, served as the primary multilateral framework for negotiating a peaceful settlement. Over more than three decades, it produced several near-agreements—including the 2001 Key West talks and various iterations of the "Madrid Principles"—but none achieved ratification. The framework rested on three core elements: the return of occupied territories surrounding Nagorno-Karabakh to Azerbaijani jurisdiction, interim arrangements for the status of the enclave itself, and a future referendum on final status. Armenia and Azerbaijan remained irreconcilably divided over the sequencing of these elements. The 2020 war effectively bypassed the Minsk framework entirely; Russia brokered the November 9 ceasefire unilaterally, and the 2023 Azerbaijani offensive dissolved whatever remained of Minsk Group credibility. Azerbaijan subsequently called for the group's dissolution, and France's deteriorating relations with Baku rendered the framework moribund.

The humanitarian dimensions of the conflict attracted sustained attention from UN agencies, particularly following the 2023 offensive. The International Court of Justice had issued provisional measures orders in February 2022, instructing Azerbaijan to ensure unimpeded movement through the Lachin corridor—the sole land connection between Armenia and the remaining Armenian population in Nagorno-Karabakh. Azerbaijan's nine-month blockade of the corridor from December 2022 to September 2023, which preceded the final offensive, was documented by the ICRC and OHCHR as causing severe shortages of food, medicine, and fuel for the civilian population. After the September 2023 offensive and the mass exodus of over 100,000 Armenians, the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights expressed serious concern and called for independent international access to the region. The UNHCR established large-scale operations in Armenia to assist the displaced population, recording over 100,600 arrivals within days. Azerbaijan's continued refusal to permit independent human rights monitoring in Nagorno-Karabakh has impeded any definitive international assessment of conditions on the ground.

At the systemic level, the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict exemplifies the unresolved tension within international law between two foundational principles: the right of peoples to self-determination and the territorial integrity of sovereign states. The Security Council's four resolutions placed the international community firmly on the side of Azerbaijani territorial integrity, a position consistent with the UN's general reluctance to endorse unilateral secession. Yet the forced displacement of virtually the entire Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh—an outcome that occurred without meaningful international intervention—has generated searching questions about the adequacy of existing conflict resolution mechanisms and the responsibility to protect civilians in recognized crisis situations. The collapse of the OSCE Minsk Group and the failure of Russian peacekeepers to uphold their mandate have prompted calls from scholars and practitioners alike for structural reforms in how international institutions manage protracted territorial disputes, particularly in regions where great-power rivalries limit Security Council action.
