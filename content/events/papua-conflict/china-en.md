China's position on the West Papua conflict is unambiguous: Indonesia's sovereignty and territorial integrity over the territory are fully supported, and the question is categorically treated as a domestic Indonesian matter not subject to international intervention. This stance flows directly from the foundational principle of Chinese foreign policy — non-interference in the internal affairs of sovereign states — which Beijing applies universally as a matter of both principle and self-interest. For China, publicly recognizing any legitimacy in secessionist claims would create unwanted precedent for its own internal governance challenges in Tibet, Xinjiang, and Taiwan. Chinese official statements and state media coverage of Papua consistently frame Indonesian administration as legitimate and portray independence advocacy as foreign-instigated destabilization.

China's economic engagement with Indonesia has expanded dramatically since the 2000s, giving Beijing a strong material incentive to maintain stable relations with Jakarta. Indonesia is among China's most important trading partners, and the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has channeled substantial Chinese investment into Indonesian infrastructure — including industrial parks, transportation networks, and energy projects. The Morowali Industrial Park in Sulawesi, a major Chinese-backed nickel processing complex, exemplifies the scale of Chinese economic penetration in the archipelago. Papua itself, with its abundant deposits of gold, copper, nickel, and hydrocarbons, represents a significant resource frontier. While direct Chinese participation in Papuan resource extraction has been limited compared to American-controlled Freeport-McMoRan, Chinese state-owned enterprises have engaged with Indonesian counterparts on infrastructure and resource processing that indirectly supports extraction operations.

In diplomatic terms, China's relationship with Indonesia anchors its broader Southeast Asia strategy. Indonesia, as the de facto leader of ASEAN, the world's fourth most populous country, and a major emerging economy, is indispensable to Chinese regional influence. Despite intermittent friction over China's expansive claims in the South China Sea — which overlap with Indonesia's Natuna Islands exclusive economic zone — both governments have managed these disputes through quiet negotiation while deepening economic interdependence. Criticizing Indonesian Papua policy would jeopardize this strategically vital relationship while simultaneously undermining the non-interference doctrine that China invokes to shield itself from international criticism. If Western states or Pacific Island nations apply pressure on Indonesia over Papua, China positions itself as a counterweight, framing such intervention as neo-colonial interference and double standards.

China's intensifying engagement with Pacific Island nations has created a latent tension with its Indonesia-first posture on Papua. Countries such as Vanuatu and the Solomon Islands — both of which have been targets of Chinese diplomatic and infrastructure investment — are among the most vocal advocates for West Papuan self-determination at the UN. China navigates this contradiction carefully, accepting Pacific Island positions on Papua as expressions of sovereign foreign policy without amplifying or endorsing them, and insulating this issue from its bilateral discussions with Jakarta. Beijing's approach reflects a broader pattern: on territorial and sovereignty questions where Chinese interests are most engaged, the non-interference principle is applied rigidly; where the contradictions are uncomfortable, they are managed through deliberate ambiguity. The overall result is that China plays no constructive role in international efforts to address the Papua conflict, serving instead as a diplomatic shield for Indonesian sovereignty within multilateral forums.
