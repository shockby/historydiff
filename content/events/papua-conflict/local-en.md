The 1969 "Act of Free Choice" (Pepera) stands as the foundational grievance of West Papuan indigenous peoples. The transfer of administration from the Netherlands to Indonesia had already been imposed in 1962–1963 through the New York Agreement, negotiated without Papuan representation. The Pepera itself was not a general referendum: rather than allowing universal suffrage among West Papua's approximately 800,000 inhabitants, Indonesian authorities handpicked just 1,025 representatives who voted unanimously for integration with Indonesia. West Papuans bitterly call this the "Act of No Choice." UN General Assembly Resolution 2504 (1969) merely "took note" of the result — a carefully chosen phrase that international legal scholars broadly interpret as falling short of explicit endorsement of legitimacy. The right of self-determination, enshrined in the UN Charter and subsequent covenants, was never meaningfully exercised by the Papuan people.

Since Indonesia assumed full control, indigenous Papuans report systematic human rights violations. The Indonesian military (TNI) has conducted repeated counter-insurgency operations against the Free Papua Movement (Organisasi Papua Merdeka, OPM) and its successor factions. These operations have been documented by international human rights organizations to include extrajudicial killings, torture, arbitrary detention, and enforced disappearances. Access to Papua by foreign journalists, diplomats, and researchers is heavily restricted — a policy that, critics argue, serves to conceal abuses from international scrutiny. Even after Indonesia's democratic transition in 1998 (Reformasi), Papua remains subject to special security restrictions. The display of the Morning Star independence flag can result in criminal prosecution, and peaceful pro-independence activists face lengthy prison sentences under broad anti-treason statutes.

The transmigration policy (transmigrasi) has reshaped Papua's demographic landscape in ways indigenous communities describe as a form of cultural and demographic erasure. From the 1960s through the 1990s, hundreds of thousands of settlers from Java, Bali, and other overcrowded islands were relocated to Papua under government-sponsored programs. As a result, indigenous Melanesian Papuans — who made up the vast majority of the population in the early 1960s — now constitute an estimated 40–50% of the provincial population by some analyses. Indigenous communities have lost customary land to settlers and state-allocated concessions, while remaining largely excluded from the economic benefits of Papua's immense natural resources. The Grasberg mine, operated by Freeport-McMoRan, is the world's largest gold mine and one of the largest copper mines, yet surrounding indigenous communities report environmental devastation of rivers and forests alongside persistent poverty.

The contemporary independence movement — represented internationally by bodies such as the United Liberation Movement for West Papua (ULMWP) — continues to press its case before regional and international forums. The Melanesian Spearhead Group (MSG) granted the ULMWP observer status in 2015, reflecting solidarity among Melanesian peoples across the Pacific. Nations including Vanuatu and the Solomon Islands have raised the issue at the UN General Assembly. Indonesia's 2022 administrative reorganization, which divided what had been two Papua provinces into six, was denounced by indigenous leaders as a divide-and-rule strategy to fragment political organization and open new resource concessions rather than a genuine devolution of power. For the West Papuan independence movement, the core demand remains unchanged: a genuine, internationally supervised act of self-determination — the vote that was denied in 1969.
