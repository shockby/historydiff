The United Nations occupies an uncomfortable and contested position in the West Papua issue, having been both a formal participant in the territory's decolonization process and a largely passive observer of its aftermath. The 1962 New York Agreement — brokered by the UN — transferred West Papua from Dutch colonial administration to a temporary UN authority (UNTEA) before handing it to Indonesia, with an explicit obligation for Indonesia to conduct a genuine "Act of Free Choice" by 1969. The UN deployed a representative, Ortiz Sanz, to observe the Pepera, who reportedly expressed reservations about the modalities of the vote — particularly that 1,025 handpicked representatives, rather than the general Papuan population, cast the ballots. Nevertheless, UN General Assembly Resolution 2504 (November 1969) closed the matter by "taking note" of the result, a deliberately ambiguous formulation. Most international legal scholars interpret "took note" as distinct from an explicit endorsement of legitimacy, though Indonesia and many states have treated it as conclusive. This ambiguity remains legally and politically unresolved.

On human rights, the international community's engagement has been persistently constrained by Indonesian restrictions on access to Papua. The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) has repeatedly requested authorization to conduct independent monitoring missions in Papua, requests that Indonesia has declined or indefinitely deferred. The Human Rights Council's Universal Periodic Review (UPR) mechanism has provided the most consistent international forum: during Indonesia's reviews in 2012, 2017, and 2022, numerous member states issued recommendations urging improved human rights conditions in Papua, including freedom of expression, press access, and investigation of reported killings. Indonesia has accepted some recommendations while classifying others — particularly those touching on accountability for military operations — as interference in domestic affairs.

The tension between the principle of self-determination and the principle of territorial integrity runs throughout the international community's approach to Papua. Both are foundational norms of the UN Charter, and their reconciliation in specific cases has long been a source of legal controversy. The 1970 Declaration on Principles of International Law acknowledged that self-determination could override territorial integrity in cases of colonial or racist regimes, a provision that Papuan advocates argue applies here. However, the dominant trend in UN practice has been to favor territorial integrity once a state's sovereignty over a territory has been internationally recognized — and Indonesia's control over Papua has been effectively recognized by the majority of UN member states since 1969. The G77 bloc, ASEAN states, and much of the Global South have generally supported Indonesian sovereignty, limiting the political space for UN action.

In recent years, however, attention has incrementally grown. Pacific Island nations — particularly Vanuatu, the Solomon Islands, and Tuvalu — have repeatedly raised West Papua at the UN General Assembly, invoking Melanesian solidarity and unresolved decolonization obligations. The UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (UNPFII) and the Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples have examined aspects of the Papua situation under the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007), which Indonesia voted against before eventually endorsing. The 2022 splitting of Papua into six provinces drew international scrutiny, with critics arguing it violated customary consultation requirements for decisions affecting indigenous peoples. Despite growing awareness, the UN system as a whole has yet to exercise decisive leverage on the Papua conflict, reflecting the broader tension between state-centric international order and emerging norms of indigenous rights and accountability.
