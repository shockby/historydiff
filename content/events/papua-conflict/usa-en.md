The United States bears significant historical responsibility for the outcome of the West Papua question, a fact that has become more widely acknowledged in subsequent decades. During the Kennedy and Johnson administrations, Washington actively backed the transfer of West Papua to Indonesia as a Cold War strategic calculation: Indonesia under Sukarno, and later Suharto, was seen as a critical bulwark against communist expansion in Southeast Asia. The 1962 New York Agreement — which facilitated Dutch withdrawal and Indonesian takeover — was brokered with strong US pressure on the Netherlands to relinquish the territory. American diplomatic cables and declassified documents confirm that US officials were aware of the deeply flawed nature of the 1969 Pepera but chose not to challenge the result, prioritizing the broader relationship with Suharto's anti-communist New Order government over Papuan self-determination. The subsequent disclosure of these decisions has informed critical assessments in the English-speaking academic and journalistic communities.

American media and civil society have periodically brought West Papua to broader public attention. Major outlets including The New York Times, The Guardian, and the BBC have published investigative reporting on Indonesian military operations, mining-related environmental destruction, and the suppression of the independence movement. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International — both primarily English-language organizations — have published the most comprehensive documentation of abuses in Papua available internationally. The US State Department's annual Country Reports on Human Rights Practices consistently notes restrictions on freedom of expression, assembly, and press access in Papua, as well as reports of security force abuses. Congressional interest has been sporadic but recurring: some members of Congress have introduced resolutions calling for human rights monitoring in Papua, though none have advanced to binding legislation.

US strategic policy toward Indonesia has evolved through different phases but has generally maintained the same fundamental approach: prioritizing the bilateral relationship over direct pressure on Papua. During the Cold War, this meant near-unconditional support for Suharto despite well-documented repression. After the 1991 Santa Cruz massacre in East Timor and Indonesia's subsequent democratization, the US imposed temporary military restrictions and ultimately supported East Timorese independence in 1999 — a precedent that West Papuan advocates frequently invoke. However, policymakers have generally treated East Timor as an exception enabled by unique circumstances rather than a template. In the contemporary Indo-Pacific competition with China, Indonesia's strategic significance as the world's largest archipelago state and a key non-aligned actor has, if anything, reinforced American reluctance to apply pressure on sensitive internal issues.

The English-speaking world's relationship with the Papua issue is shaped by the contrast between its stated values — democracy, human rights, and self-determination — and its geopolitical calculations. Australia, as the former colonial administrator of the eastern half of the island and Papua New Guinea's closest partner, maintains its own complex position: acknowledging Papuan concerns in rhetoric while avoiding anything that would destabilize relations with Indonesia, its most important regional neighbor. Academic scholarship in English — particularly from Australian and American universities — has produced some of the most rigorous analyses of the Papua conflict's legal and political dimensions, including works by scholars such as Robert Elson, Richard Chauvel, and John Saltford. This body of work has contributed to a growing consensus in Western academic circles that the 1969 Act of Free Choice was fundamentally illegitimate, even as Western governments have declined to act on that assessment.
