China's position on the Preah Vihear dispute has been one of de facto alignment with Cambodia, framed through the language of non-interference and respect for sovereignty rather than explicit legal advocacy. This stance is most coherently understood within the architecture of the "Comprehensive Strategic Partnership" between Beijing and Phnom Penh—a relationship that deepened substantially through the 2000s and accelerated precisely during the period of heightened border tensions. While China officially professed adherence to ASEAN's principle of non-interference in bilateral disputes, its expanding aid, investment, and military cooperation with Cambodia effectively provided Phnom Penh with the economic security and diplomatic backing necessary to maintain a strong position in its confrontation with Bangkok. Between 2008 and 2013, Chinese aid to Cambodia surpassed one billion U.S. dollars annually, making Beijing by far the largest bilateral donor.

From the perspective articulated by Chinese officials and state media, the 1962 ICJ judgment represents a binding international legal determination that should be respected—a framing that implicitly endorsed Cambodia's position without requiring explicit condemnation of Thailand. Chinese Foreign Ministry spokespersons consistently invoked the formula of "respect for sovereignty, non-interference, and peaceful dialogue" in responding to queries about the dispute, language that offered rhetorical support to Phnom Penh while preserving diplomatic deniability. Critics have noted the selective character of this stance: China simultaneously dismissed the 2016 South China Sea Permanent Court of Arbitration ruling as "null and void," suggesting that Chinese declarations of support for international legal decisions are calibrated to align with strategic interests rather than principled commitment to adjudication.

The 2012 ASEAN Ministerial Meeting in Phnom Penh—at which Cambodia, then serving as ASEAN Chair, blocked consensus on a joint communiqué addressing the South China Sea—is widely cited as the most visible illustration of how Cambodia's dependence on Chinese patronage translated into diplomatic behavior at the regional level. While that episode concerned a separate dispute, it demonstrated the degree to which China's strategic partnership with Cambodia could shape ASEAN's collective output. In the Preah Vihear context, analysts from Southeast Asian and Western research institutions have argued that Chinese backing emboldened Phnom Penh to resist Thai pressure and to pursue the UNESCO World Heritage nomination more aggressively than it might have otherwise, on the calculation that Beijing's support buffered any potential economic consequences from Thai displeasure.

China's broader strategic interest in the Mekong sub-region provides the geopolitical framework within which its engagement with the Preah Vihear dispute is most fully intelligible. Beijing's infrastructure investment corridor linking Yunnan province southward through Laos, Thailand, and Cambodia to the Gulf of Thailand places a high strategic premium on stable, friendly relations with Cambodia. The Lancang-Mekong Cooperation framework, launched by China in 2015, further institutionalised Beijing's claim to a coordinating role in mainland Southeast Asia. For China, therefore, the Preah Vihear dispute represented not merely a bilateral heritage controversy but a touchstone of alliance reliability—an opportunity to demonstrate to Cambodia and to other smaller Southeast Asian states that alignment with China carries tangible diplomatic dividends, even when confronting a fellow ASEAN member that is also a longstanding U.S. partner.
