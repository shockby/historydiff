For the communities on both sides of the Thai-Cambodian border, the Preah Vihear temple—known in Thai as Khao Phra Wihan—has long held a significance that far transcends any political or territorial claim. To Cambodians living in Preah Vihear province, the 11th-century Khmer temple represents a sacred link to the civilization of Angkor and a living site of Buddhist and Hindu worship. To Thai villagers in the Sisaket and Surin provinces just across the border, the site was equally venerated, and for decades before the dispute intensified, modest cross-border religious and commercial exchanges were commonplace. The human geography of the border region was defined less by nationalist demarcation than by shared spiritual heritage and mutual economic dependence.

This fragile coexistence was shattered when UNESCO's 2008 World Heritage inscription of Preah Vihear—submitted by Cambodia without Thai consent—ignited a diplomatic crisis that rapidly militarized. As both armies deployed along the contested frontier, residents of the buffer zone found themselves caught between national armies prosecuting a dispute over which they had no voice. Clashes between 2008 and 2011 involved small-arms fire, artillery exchanges, and land mines detonated in areas where villagers farmed and children walked to school. The United Nations estimated that up to 80,000 Cambodians were internally displaced at the height of the conflict, while Thai villages in proximity to the contested 4.6 km² area also saw significant evacuations.

Testimonies gathered by NGOs including the Cambodian Human Rights and Development Association (ADHOC) paint a sobering picture of civilian vulnerability. Farmers on both sides described abandoning crops at harvest time, fleeing with little more than they could carry, and returning to find fields rendered impassable by unexploded ordnance. The loss of access to the temple also severed an important source of tourism income for nearby Cambodian communities. Perhaps most painfully, residents who maintained personal or familial ties across the border found those connections severed by military deployments and new travel restrictions. The dispute reinforced the very national divisions that ordinary life in the border zone had long blurred.

The 2013 ICJ clarification ruling and subsequent diplomatic thaw allowed for gradual resumption of cross-border activity, but the human costs of the conflict remain inadequately addressed. Land-mine contamination continues to restrict agricultural livelihoods; no comprehensive reparations framework has been established for those displaced; and tenure rights in the 4.6 km² area remain legally ambiguous for residents who lived there prior to the hostilities. For the people who actually inhabit this landscape, enduring peace will require not only the legal certainty that international courts can provide, but also concrete investment in demining, resettlement support, and the revival of the cross-border cultural exchanges that once made this contested border a place of meeting rather than conflict.
