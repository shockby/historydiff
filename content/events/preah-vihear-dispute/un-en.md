The International Court of Justice's landmark judgment of 15 June 1962 established in international law that sovereignty over the Temple of Preah Vihear belongs to Cambodia. The Court's ruling—adopted by a majority of nine votes to three—rested on a careful analysis of the boundary instruments concluded during the French colonial era, particularly the 1904 Treaty between France and Siam and the maps produced by the Franco-Siamese Mixed Commission of 1907. Critically, the Court found that while the Annex I map did not perfectly follow the watershed line stipulated as the boundary criterion in the treaty, Thailand had accepted the map through decades of conduct, including official visits to the temple and reliance on the map in communications with third parties. The principle of acquiescence thus bound Thailand to the cartographic line placing the temple on Cambodian soil. The Court ordered Thailand to withdraw its forces and return any objects removed from the temple—a judgment that represents a foundational precedent in international boundary law.

UNESCO's inscription of Preah Vihear as a World Heritage Site in July 2008 reignited a dispute widely considered resolved. Cambodia had submitted its nomination unilaterally after bilateral consultations with Thailand stalled; Thailand argued that the inscription—and the management zone accompanying it—effectively prejudged the outstanding boundary dispute in Cambodia's favour. UNESCO's institutional position emphasised that cultural heritage designations are made on the basis of outstanding universal value and operate independently of sovereignty disputes, but acknowledged the unprecedented diplomatic strain. The World Heritage Committee, in subsequent sessions, requested Cambodia to revise its management plan and engage Thailand in joint heritage stewardship—an approach that encapsulates UNESCO's broader philosophy of using cultural cooperation to bridge political divisions.

On 11 November 2013, the ICJ delivered its interpretation of the 1962 judgment in response to Cambodia's 2011 request for clarification. By fifteen votes to zero, the Court held that the 1962 judgment had determined that the whole of the promontory of Preah Vihear—not merely the temple structures—fell within Cambodian territory, and that Thailand was consequently under an obligation to withdraw all its personnel from that zone. The ruling definitively rejected Thailand's narrower reading of the 1962 judgment, which had confined Cambodian sovereignty to the temple buildings themselves. The Court notably declined, however, to fix the precise limits of the promontory or the 4.6 km² disputed area, holding that boundary delimitation as such lay beyond the scope of the original judgment's operative clause and must be resolved through bilateral negotiation or further legal proceedings.

The United Nations Security Council's response to the 2008 and 2011 military clashes was limited to presidential statements urging maximum restraint, stopping well short of binding resolutions or sanctions. Indonesia's 2011 proposal, in its capacity as ASEAN Chair, to deploy an international observer team was rejected by Thailand on sovereignty grounds, illustrating the structural constraints that regional organisations face when member states invoke non-interference norms. These episodes are widely studied in international relations as examples of the enforcement gap in international law: while the ICJ can authoritatively settle legal questions, the implementation of its judgments ultimately depends on the political will of the parties and the willingness of the broader international community to apply sustained diplomatic pressure.
