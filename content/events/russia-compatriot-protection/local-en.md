Ukraine, Georgia and Moldova argue that “protecting compatriots” is a pretext for military intervention and carving off territory. They say there was no systematic persecution of Russian speakers in Crimea, and Kyiv treats the 2022 invasion as a war of aggression against its sovereignty and territorial integrity. Many Russian speakers in Ukraine fought to defend the country, and Russian-speaking eastern and southern cities suffered the worst destruction.

Georgia says Russia mass-distributed passports to residents of South Ossetia and Abkhazia from around 2002 (“passportization”) and then used “protecting its citizens” as justification. The two regions are about 20% of Georgian territory and Tbilisi still calls them occupied. Moldova likewise demands the withdrawal of roughly 1,500 Russian troops from Transnistria as a violation of its sovereignty, and its constitution proclaims neutrality.

The Baltic states regard the 1940 Soviet annexation as an occupation and argue that the legal status of Russian speakers is a citizenship-law matter, with naturalization open. In Latvia debate continues over a large stateless “non-citizen” population. They view the 2007 Bronze Soldier riots in Tallinn and the cyberattacks as precedents of Russian interference, worry since 2014 about a “next target” such as the border town of Narva, and rely on NATO's Article 5.

In Kazakhstan, alarm grew after Putin said in 2014 that Kazakhs “had never had statehood” and after Russian lawmakers in 2020 suggested the north was a Russian “gift”. Kazakhstan did not recognize the “independence” of Donetsk and Luhansk, and stresses that the rights of ethnic Russians are protected by its own laws and multiethnic policy.
