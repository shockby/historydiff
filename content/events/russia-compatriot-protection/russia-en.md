In the Russian government's account, the Soviet collapse suddenly left some 25 million ethnic Russians and Russian speakers outside Russia. Under the 1999 law on compatriots abroad and the 2020 constitutional amendments (Article 69), Moscow says it must defend their rights and interests. It frames this not as territorial expansion but as a humanitarian duty to protect them from nationalism, discrimination and violence.

Key examples: in the 1992 Transnistria conflict Russia's 14th Army intervened, and Russian “peacekeepers” have remained since. In the 2008 Georgia war Russia described its action as “peace enforcement” to protect Russian citizens in South Ossetia from Georgian attack. For Crimea in 2014 it cited a self-determination referendum and the need to protect Russian speakers from Ukraine's new government, which Moscow calls the product of a coup.

For the full-scale invasion of February 24, 2022, President Putin said Donbas residents had faced “genocide” for eight years and listed protecting them among the aims of the “special military operation”. Russia stresses security concerns over NATO enlargement, and alleged suppression of the Russian language and culture in Ukraine.

Russia has also repeatedly condemned, at the UN and OSCE, the stateless “non-citizen” status of many Russians in Latvia and Estonia and the reduction of Russian-language schooling as discrimination. Policy toward minorities inside Russia is, in its view, a domestic matter closed to foreign interference.
