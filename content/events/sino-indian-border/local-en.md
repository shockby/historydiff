For the inhabitants of Ladakh, the Sino-Indian border dispute is not a distant diplomatic abstraction but a lived reality that shapes daily subsistence and mobility. Once a flourishing crossroads of Central Asian, Tibetan, and South Asian trade routes, Ladakh has effectively become a militarized frontier zone since 1962. The Line of Actual Control (LAC) has bisected grazing lands that nomadic communities—Changpas and others—have used for generations. Following the June 2020 Galwan Valley clash, expanded buffer zones established by the Indian military further curtailed access to ancestral pastures, particularly in the Depsang plains and the Fingers area along Pangong Lake's northern bank, where military installations and surveillance infrastructure have encroached on traditional pastoral corridors.

Residents of Arunachal Pradesh carry Indian citizenship and political identity while living under the shadow of China's persistent claim to their homeland as "Zangnan" (South Tibet). Communities such as the Monpa people of the Tawang district maintain deep cultural continuity with Tibetan Buddhism, language, and customs while identifying politically with the Indian state. Of particular concern to local residents is China's recurring practice of renaming landmarks and settlements within Arunachal Pradesh—issuing lists of "standardized" Chinese place names (six in 2017, fifteen in 2021, and more subsequently)—which communities experience as an attempt to erase indigenous and local historical identities and assert cartographic claims through nomenclature.

The political situation of Ladakhi residents was further complicated by the 2019 revocation of Jammu & Kashmir's special status and the creation of the Union Territory of Ladakh. While some welcomed the change as offering direct Central government attention, civic leaders—including the Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Councils of Leh and Kargil—have mounted sustained campaigns for Sixth Schedule protections under the Indian Constitution, which would safeguard tribal land rights and offer insulation against demographic change. The rapid infrastructure push in border zones, while strategically significant, is viewed ambivalently: roads and bridges arrive without commensurate investment in schools, healthcare, and local economic diversification.

The emotional landscape of border communities is marked by a complex interplay of patriotic commitment and quiet resentment at being instrumentalized by distant state powers. Younger Ladakhis increasingly enlist in the Indian armed forces and express pride in national service, while older herders mourn the loss of trans-boundary mobility that once characterized their pastoral economy. Tourism—a vital income source for the Ladakhi economy—has been severely curtailed in sensitive border areas since 2020. What unites most border-area voices across linguistic and ethnic lines is a desire to have their land rights, cultural practices, and economic livelihoods formally recognized and protected before the final contours of any diplomatic settlement are drawn.
