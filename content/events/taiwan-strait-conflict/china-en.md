Beijing holds that Taiwan has been an inseparable part of Chinese territory since ancient times, and that the Taiwan question is the unfinished business of the civil war that began in 1949 — a purely internal affair. It points to centuries of ties with the mainland, Qing rule from 1683, and Taiwan’s cession to Japan under the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki, which it says was reversed when the Cairo Declaration (1943) and the Potsdam Declaration (1945) returned the island to China.

In China’s account, the People’s Republic established in 1949 became the sole legitimate government of China, reducing the Republic of China to a “local authority”. Beijing cites UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 (1971) and the “one-China” recognition given by more than 180 countries, including the United States and Japan, when they established ties. “Peaceful reunification and one country, two systems” is the stated policy, but the 2005 Anti-Secession Law provides for “non-peaceful means” should Taiwan secede.

Chinese textbooks present separatism and foreign interference as the greatest obstacle to national rejuvenation, and reunification as essential to it. Beijing condemns the Democratic Progressive Party administration as a “pro-independence force” while calling for “integrated development” through exchanges and economic ties with ordinary Taiwanese. Military exercises are described as a “stern warning to separatists and outside interference”.
