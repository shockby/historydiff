The government of Taiwan (the Republic of China) argues that Taiwan has never been part of the People’s Republic of China, and is a sovereign state with its own government, military, currency and passports. The ROC was founded in 1912 and took over Taiwan in 1945 after Japan’s surrender; it has continued to exist since moving there after losing the civil war in 1949. The Democratic Progressive Party’s official position (the 1999 Resolution on Taiwan’s Future) is that because the ROC (Taiwan) is already a sovereign, independent country, there is no need to declare independence.

Taiwanese textbooks and scholarship stress a “multicultural history” — Dutch and Spanish rule, the Zheng regime, Qing rule, Japanese rule (1895–1945) and the postwar Kuomintang authoritarian era (the 1947 February 28 Incident and 38 years of martial law) — and present Taiwan as a historical subject in its own right rather than the periphery of Chinese history. Democratisation, via the lifting of martial law in 1987 and the first direct presidential election in 1996, is a source of national pride and the core difference from the PRC’s system.

Opinion polls show roughly six in ten residents identify as “Taiwanese only”, while very few identify as “Chinese only”. Yet most people prefer neither immediate independence nor unification but the “status quo”. Taipei says it is open to dialogue but rejects “one country, two systems” and any unification on terms that would undermine its democracy and sovereignty.
