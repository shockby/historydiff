The United States established diplomatic relations with the People’s Republic of China and severed ties with Taiwan (the ROC) in 1979. In the same year it enacted the Taiwan Relations Act, which provides for the sale of defensive arms to Taiwan and states that the US regards any use of force against Taiwan with “grave concern”. The Act does not oblige the US military to defend Taiwan, which is the basis of so-called “strategic ambiguity”.

The US “one China policy” is distinct from Beijing’s “one China principle”. In the three joint communiqués (1972, 1979, 1982) the US “acknowledges” China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, without endorsing or rejecting it. The “Six Assurances” given to Taipei in 1982 (including no end date for arms sales and no mediation between Taipei and Beijing) are another pillar. Washington says Taiwan’s future should be resolved peacefully, in a way acceptable to people on both sides of the Strait, and opposes unilateral changes to the status quo.

In recent years, as China’s military has grown, the US has increased arms sales and Taiwan Strait transits, and former President Biden said several times that US forces would defend Taiwan (each time followed by clarification that policy had not changed). Washington also stresses the importance of Taiwan’s semiconductor industry and sees a Taiwan contingency as the greatest risk in US–China relations. A debate continues in the US over whether to keep ambiguity or make an explicit defence commitment.
