**UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 (adopted October 25, 1971; 76 in favor, 35 against, 17 abstentions)** is the central document in the international debate surrounding the current Taiwan issue. The gap between its content and its interpretations is itself the crucial "diff."

**What the resolution text actually says:**
"Recognizing that the representatives of the Government of the People's Republic of China are the only lawful representatives of China to the United Nations and that the People's Republic of China is one of the five permanent members of the Security Council. Decides to restore all its rights to the People's Republic of China and to recognize the representatives of its Government as the only lawful representatives of China to the United Nations... and to expel forthwith the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek from the place which they unlawfully occupy at the United Nations."

**What the resolution does NOT say:**
Nowhere in the resolution text does it state that "Taiwan is part of the People's Republic of China" or that "Taiwan's attribution belongs to the People's Republic of China." The resolution resolved the question of "China's representation" — it did not resolve "Taiwan's sovereign status."

**Why this "what it doesn't say" matters:**
China cites this resolution as the basis for "the international establishment of the One China Principle," but Taiwan, the United States, and some European scholars criticize this interpretation as an "ultra vires reading of the resolution." At the same time, the reality exists that the vast majority of the international community (180+ countries) maintains diplomatic relations only with the PRC and does not formally recognize Taiwan as a sovereign state.

The gap between "what the resolution text actually means" and "how countries interpret and act upon it" is one of the core issues in the Taiwan question, and is cited internationally as an example of how UN resolutions are built upon "politically agreed ambiguity."
