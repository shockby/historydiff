The position of the United States officially adopts a "One China Policy," but this is intentionally distinguished from China's "One China Principle."

In the 1972 "Shanghai Communiqué" during Nixon's visit to China, the U.S. "acknowledged" (but did not "recognize") the Chinese position that "all Chinese on either side of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is but one China and that Taiwan is a part of China." In 1979, the U.S. established diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China while simultaneously enacting the Taiwan Relations Act, which legally mandated the provision of defensive articles to Taiwan and codified U.S. interest in Taiwan's security and stability.

The core of the U.S. policy of "strategic ambiguity" is to not explicitly state Taiwan's final status, thereby simultaneously achieving deterrence against PRC military unification and restraint of Taiwan's unilateral declaration of independence. Under the Biden administration, statements that "the U.S. would defend Taiwan if China attacked" were made on multiple occasions, but the White House each time issued corrections that "there is no change in policy." While the U.S. supports Taiwan's participation in international organizations, it does not officially support Taiwan's independence and maintains "preservation of the status quo" as its fundamental policy.
