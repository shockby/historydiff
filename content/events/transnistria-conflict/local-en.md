For the predominantly Russian-speaking population of the Pridnestrovian Moldavian Republic (PMR), the roots of the conflict trace back to the final years of the Soviet Union, when Moldova's declaration of sovereignty in 1990 was accompanied by legislation making Romanian (referred to by Moldovans as "Moldovan") the sole state language and replacing the Cyrillic script with the Latin alphabet. Russian-speaking communities—comprising ethnic Russians, Ukrainians, and Russian-assimilated Moldovans—feared cultural marginalization and the loss of decades of Soviet-era linguistic and institutional heritage. PMR declared independence on September 2, 1990, and following the armed conflict of 1992, has maintained de facto self-governance ever since. With its capital in Tiraspol, the PMR operates its own constitution, currency (the Transnistrian ruble), military, and passport system, and continues to display Soviet iconography including Lenin statues and the hammer-and-sickle on its flag. Many residents hold Russian passports and receive Russian state pensions, cementing practical ties to Moscow.

From the PMR perspective, the 1992 War of Transnistria was initiated by Moldovan security forces and volunteer units attempting to assert Chișinău's authority by force, causing significant civilian casualties on the left bank of the Dniester. Russia's 14th Army—now reconstituted as the Operational Group of Russian Forces (OGRF)—is credited by PMR residents with halting the fighting and ensuring the ceasefire of July 21, 1992. This military presence is thus perceived not as foreign occupation but as a legitimate peacekeeping guarantee. The PMR's economy is shaped by the Sheriff Group, a sprawling conglomerate that controls retail, telecommunications, media, and even the region's top football club (FC Sheriff Tiraspol). State stability and relatively predictable public services—partly underpinned by cheap Russian natural gas—have historically been cited by residents as justifications for maintaining the status quo over reunification with Moldova.

From the Moldovan perspective on the right bank of the Dniester, Transnistria remains an illegally occupied portion of the sovereign Republic of Moldova whose territorial integrity is affirmed by the 1994 Moldovan Constitution and recognized by the United Nations. Veterans and bereaved families of the 1992 war continue to mark the conflict's anniversaries, and the unresolved status of the region is felt as a permanent wound on the national body. Moldovan-language (Romanian-language) schools operating within Transnistria have faced persistent administrative obstacles, and ethnic Moldovans in the region report limited economic mobility. The European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) ruled in Ilaşcu and Others v. Moldova and Russia (2004) that Russia bears continued responsibility for human rights violations in the PMR. Moldova's government has proposed multiple reunification frameworks offering Transnistria a "special status" within a federal or asymmetric structure, but substantive progress has repeatedly stalled.

The security calculus shifted dramatically after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Moldova's border with Ukraine runs adjacent to Transnistria, and a series of unexplained explosions in the region in April 2022 heightened fears of deliberate destabilization. When Ukraine terminated the transit of Russian natural gas at the start of 2024, Transnistria—which had relied on heavily subsidized Russian gas—faced an acute energy crisis, with residents experiencing heating shortages throughout the winter months. The PMR parliament issued a formal appeal to Russia requesting "protection" in early 2024, though no concrete Russian response materialized. Against this backdrop, younger PMR residents are increasingly mobile, with significant numbers emigrating to Romania, Moldova proper, or Russia, leaving the region with a shrinking and aging population uncertain about its future.
