Victims' families and human rights advocates in Northern Ireland argue that British state forces operated with near-total impunity throughout the Troubles—from **internment without trial (1971)**, the **Ballymurphy massacre** (11 civilians killed in 1971, all found entirely innocent by a 2021 coroner's inquest), and **Bloody Sunday (1972)** to documented collusion with loyalist paramilitaries. Both nationalist and unionist parties, alongside victims' groups and the Irish government, condemned the **2023 Legacy Act** as a de facto amnesty designed to shield British veterans and paramilitaries from prosecution—a critique upheld in 2024 when the Belfast High Court and Court of Appeal ruled the Act's conditional immunity provisions incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR). In **Scotland**, pro-independence parties led by the Scottish National Party (SNP) contend that Brexit—which **62%** of Scottish voters opposed in 2016—fundamentally altered the constitutional premise of the 2014 referendum, and that Westminster's refusal to sanction a new vote denies Scottish self-determination.

Across former colonies, survivors and historians emphasize how Britain systematically suppressed decolonization movements and concealed the evidence. During the **Mau Mau Emergency in Kenya (1952–1960)**, British colonial authorities detained between **150,000 and over 300,000 Kikuyu** in camps Subjected to systematic torture, forced labor, and castration (while official records cited ~11,000 Mau Mau deaths, historians estimate between 20,000 and over 100,000). Only the 2011 discovery of thousands of files hidden at Hanslope Park under **Operation Legacy**—Britain's colonial-era purge of incriminating archives—enabled survivors to win the 2013 settlement.

Similarly, **Chagossians**, forcibly deported by Britain between **1968 and 1973** to clear the Chagos Archipelago for the Diego Garcia military base, criticize the 2024–2025 UK–Mauritius treaty for excluding Chagossian representatives from negotiations and continuing to bar resettlement on **Diego Garcia** itself. Meanwhile, the **CARICOM Reparations Commission** and Britain's **Windrush generation** argue that expressions of “regret” without formal state apologies or reparatory justice fail to address the enduring economic and racial legacies of transatlantic slavery and empire.
