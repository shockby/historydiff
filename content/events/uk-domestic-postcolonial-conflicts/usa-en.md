Having played a pivotal mediating role through Special Envoy George Mitchell in brokering the **1998 Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement**, the United States has treated the preservation of peace in Northern Ireland as a bipartisan foreign policy priority. Throughout the Brexit negotiations, the White House and the U.S. Congress repeatedly warned London that any arrangement creating a hard border on the island of Ireland or undermining the Good Friday Agreement would block prospects for a U.S.–UK free trade agreement, and Washington strongly welcomed the **2023 Windsor Framework**. At the same time, the **UN High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR)** and the **Council of Europe** sharply criticized the UK's **2023 Legacy Act** as a breach of Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights (the right to life and effective investigation), prompting the Irish government to file an inter-state case against the UK before the European Court of Human Rights in January 2024.

On decolonization, the **International Court of Justice (ICJ)** issued a landmark advisory opinion in **February 2019** (by 13 votes to 1) concluding that Britain's 1965 detachment of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius was unlawful and that the UK was under an obligation to end its administration “as rapidly as possible.” In May 2019, **UN General Assembly Resolution 73/295** endorsed this demand by 116 votes to 6.

For Washington, however, the paramount consideration has been the uninterrupted operation of the joint U.S.–UK military facility on **Diego Garcia**, an indispensable hub for U.S. naval and long-range bomber operations across the Middle East and the Indo-Pacific. While initially cautious about sovereignty transfer, both the Biden and second Trump administrations ultimately endorsed the 2024–2025 UK–Mauritius treaty once assured that the **99-year lease** guaranteed exclusive U.S.–UK operational control and prevented rival powers such as China from establishing any presence in the archipelago.
