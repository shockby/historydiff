The United States, the European Union, and NATO member states collectively characterize Russia's invasion of Ukraine — launched on **February 24, 2022** — as a war of aggression in violation of the UN Charter, and a fundamental challenge to the post-World War II European security order. The West frames the conflict through the lens of **democracy vs. authoritarianism**, justifying its support for Ukraine as a defense of freedom and the rule of law.

Western nations responded swiftly with sweeping sanctions against Russia. Key economic measures included: exclusion of major Russian banks from SWIFT (the international banking messaging network); freezing of Russian Central Bank foreign currency reserves (approximately \$300 billion); and the gradual reduction of energy imports from Russia. While these sanctions inflicted damage on the Russian economy, their impact has been partially offset by substitute purchases of Russian energy by India, China, and other countries.

In terms of military assistance, the United States has progressively supplied Ukraine with **Javelin anti-tank missiles**, **HIMARS multiple-launch rocket systems**, **M1 Abrams tanks**, and **ATACMS long-range missiles**. From 2024, **F-16 fighter jets** began to be transferred through a Denmark and Netherlands-led initiative. Cumulative support from NATO allies as a whole has reached tens of billions of dollars.

That said, meaningful differences in approach exist within the Western camp. Eastern European states — particularly Poland and the Baltic nations — have taken the hardest anti-Russia line, advocating for Ukraine's full and swift victory. Western European nations such as Germany and France have continued their support while expressing concern about economic fallout and the risk of nuclear escalation. In the United States, the continuity of support has become a domestic political flashpoint tied to electoral cycles, with sustained attention on whether the support framework established since 2022 will endure through 2025 and beyond.
